**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

This visualization showcases Depth-First Search (DFS) navigating the charming streets of Valparaíso's Cerro Alegre. At its core, DFS explores as deeply as possible along each branch before backtracking. When encountering a node (intersection) with multiple outgoing edges (streets), DFS arbitrarily chooses one to traverse. It doesn't consider the "cost" or "distance" of the next step like some other algorithms; it simply picks an unvisited neighbor and continues. This is typically implemented using a stack (either explicitly or implicitly through recursion) to keep track of the nodes to visit and the path taken. The "getting lost" aspect comes from its tendency to dive down long, winding paths that might not be the shortest, but its single-minded exploration can be remarkably efficient for certain problems like finding *any* path or checking connectivity.

We've transformed the intricate layout of Cerro Alegre into a graph, where every intersection represents a node and every street segment connecting them is an edge. The red lines you see highlight the edges that have been "visited" or explored by the DFS algorithm, showing its traversal path. The green lines indicate edges that have been fully explored and are now part of the backtracking process. The bright yellow line elegantly traces the final computed path found by DFS from the start to the destination. Unlike explicit grid-based graphs where every move is a standard unit, real-world road networks introduce complexities like varying street lengths, one-way streets, and intricate dead ends, all of which DFS must contend with, demonstrating its adaptability beyond simplistic scenarios.
