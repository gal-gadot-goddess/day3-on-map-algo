**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional A* 🚀**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route so fast? Here, we're visualizing the **Bidirectional A* search algorithm** in action on a real-world map of Valencia's vibrant El Cabanyal neighborhood! This isn't your typical grid-based search; we're tackling the complexities of actual urban street networks.

**How does Bidirectional A* conquer complexity?** Unlike a standard A* search that expands outwards from a single start point, Bidirectional A* runs two simultaneous searches: one forward from the start node and one backward from the goal node. Each search maintains its own open list (typically a **priority queue** implemented as a min-heap) to efficiently retrieve the node with the lowest estimated total cost (f-score). The f-score is calculated as the sum of the actual cost from the start to the current node (g-score) and the estimated heuristic cost from the current node to the goal (h-score). The key is that both searches use the *same heuristic* (e.g., Manhattan distance or Euclidean distance on the graph's node positions). When the two search frontiers meet, a path is found. This drastically reduces the search space compared to a unidirectional search, especially in large graphs, making it incredibly efficient – hence the moniker "speed king"!

**From Valencia's Streets to a Digital Graph 🗺️** Our visualization transforms El Cabanyal into a **graph**, where each street **intersection** is a **node** and each segment of **street connecting two intersections** is an **edge**. The weight of an edge represents the actual travel time or distance along that street segment. The red and green lines you see represent the **"visited edges"** of our forward and backward searches, respectively. These lines show the algorithm exploring potential routes, expanding its reach from both ends. The shimmering yellow line? That's the **optimal path** computed by Bidirectional A*, discovered when the two searches finally "meet" in the middle of the network. Implementing this on real roads is a fascinating challenge. Unlike explicit grids where node connectivity and edge weights are uniform, real-world road networks have varying street lengths, one-way restrictions, traffic patterns, and complex intersections, requiring sophisticated data structures and careful handling of edge costs and heuristic calculations.
