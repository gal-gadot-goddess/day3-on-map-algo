**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First 🗺️🚗💨**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find *a* route, even if it's not always the *optimal* one? Meet Greedy Best-First Search! This algorithm operates on a simple, yet powerful, heuristic: it prioritizes exploring paths that appear to lead *most directly* to the goal. In our visualization, you'll see it making decisions based on a heuristic function (often the straight-line or Euclidean distance) that estimates the cost to reach the target from each neighbor. Crucially, Greedy Best-First uses a **priority queue** to manage its "frontier" – the set of nodes to explore next. This priority queue ensures that the neighbor closest to the target (according to the heuristic) is always expanded first. Unlike algorithms that guarantee optimality like Dijkstra's or A*, Greedy Best-First doesn't look ahead to see if a slightly longer initial step might lead to a much shorter overall path. It's all about that immediate "best guess"!

In this real-world implementation, we've transformed the streets of Oslo's Majorstuen district in Norway into a graph! Each intersection is a node, and the roads connecting them are edges. The red and green lines you see represent "visited edges" as the algorithm explores the graph, systematically evaluating potential paths. The vibrant yellow line showcases the final path computed by Greedy Best-First, demonstrating how it zeroed in on the destination. Notice how the complexity of pathfinding on a real road network differs from a simple grid: roads aren't always uniform, one-way systems exist, and the heuristic's accuracy can be significantly influenced by actual street layouts, making the algorithm's "greedy" choices sometimes lead it down less-than-ideal detours compared to more exhaustive search strategies.
