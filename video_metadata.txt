## Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional A*

Witness the speed king in action! 👑 Our latest visualization showcases the **Bidirectional A* search algorithm** applied to the intricate street network of Barcelona's Eixample district. Unlike a single search, Bidirectional A* cleverly deploys two simultaneous searches – one starting from the origin and the other from the destination – racing towards each other. This duality significantly reduces the search space.

At its core, A* is an informed search algorithm, balancing exploration with an educated guess of future distance. Each search utilizes a **priority queue** to store nodes to be evaluated. The priority of a node is determined by `f(n) = g(n) + h(n)`, where `g(n)` is the actual cost (distance) from the start to node `n`, and `h(n)` is a heuristic estimate of the cost from node `n` to the goal. Bidirectional A* maintains separate priority queues, `openSetForward` and `openSetBackward`, and their corresponding cost maps. When the two searches meet, the algorithm has found an optimal path. Neighbor prioritization is handled by always selecting the node with the lowest `f(n)` value from the respective priority queue, ensuring the most promising paths are explored first.

Transforming the real world into a graph for this implementation is a fascinating process. In our Barcelona Eixample example, **nodes represent street intersections**, and **edges represent the streets connecting them**. Imagine a massive, interconnected web! The red and green lines you see are the "visited edges" – the exploration paths of our two simultaneous searches, one originating from the start (red) and the other from the goal (green). The vibrant yellow line is the computed shortest path, triumphantly connecting the two search frontiers. The complexity on real roads is more nuanced than on explicit grids. While grids offer uniform edge costs, real road networks often feature varying speeds, one-way streets, and complex intersection logic, making the graph representation and heuristic calculations even more critical for efficiency.
