**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route, or how search engines explore complex networks? Our latest visualization dives into the fascinating world of graph traversal with Depth-First Search (DFS) against the backdrop of **Jakarta's bustling Kebayoran Baru**. 🗺️ At its core, DFS explores a graph by going as deep as possible along each branch before backtracking. When presented with multiple neighbors at a node (an intersection, in this case), DFS doesn't analyze them all at once or prioritize based on cost. Instead, it arbitrarily picks one neighbor and **recursively** dives into its subtree. This behavior is typically managed using a **stack** (either implicitly through function call recursion or explicitly) to keep track of the current path and the nodes yet to be explored. It's a strategy that can get temporarily "lost" in long paths, but its straightforward nature often makes it remarkably efficient for certain problems.

This visualization takes the abstract concept of graph theory and grounds it in a **real-world map implementation**. We've transformed the intricate road network of Kebayoran Baru into a graph where **intersections are nodes** and **streets are edges**. The red and green lines you see represent **"visited edges"** – the paths DFS is actively exploring, marking its journey through the network. This isn't a simple grid; real-world road networks introduce complexities like one-way streets, varying road lengths, and traffic patterns, which can significantly impact the *practical* efficiency of DFS compared to theoretical analyses on explicit grids. The bright **yellow line** showcases the final **computed path** the algorithm determined. While DFS might not always yield the *shortest* path in a weighted graph (where edge costs are considered), its ability to quickly find *a* path is invaluable in many applications.
