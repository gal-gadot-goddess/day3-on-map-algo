**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how pathfinding algorithms work in the real world? Our latest visualization showcases the **Greedy Best-First** algorithm in action on a map of Santiago, specifically the Providencia commune! This algorithm prioritizes exploring nodes based on a heuristic function that estimates the cost to reach the target. Unlike algorithms like Dijkstra's which guarantee the shortest path by exhaustively exploring all options, Greedy Best-First makes a "greedy" choice at each step, moving directly towards the goal. It achieves this by using a **priority queue** to store and retrieve the most promising nodes – those closest to the target. At each iteration, it extracts the node with the lowest heuristic value from the priority queue and adds its unvisited neighbors, also prioritized by their heuristic estimates, to the queue. This direct, heuristic-driven approach makes it incredibly fast, but as you'll see, this speed can come at the cost of optimality.

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms the streets of Providencia into a **graph**. Each intersection becomes a **node**, and the streets connecting them are the **edges**. The visualization highlights this by displaying the city map. The **red and green lines** you see represent the **visited edges** – the paths explored by the algorithm as it tries to find its way. The final **yellow line** is the **computed path** determined by Greedy Best-First. It's important to note that real-world road networks are significantly more complex than simple grid-based graphs. Factors like one-way streets, varying speed limits, and complex junction layouts introduce a level of complexity that directly impacts the computational cost and the effectiveness of different pathfinding algorithms. Greedy Best-First, with its speed, can be advantageous in scenarios where quick, albeit potentially suboptimal, routes are acceptable, such as live traffic navigation or initial route suggestions.
