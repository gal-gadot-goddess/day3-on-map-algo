**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route through a sprawling city? This visualization showcases a **Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)** in action, applied to the charming streets of Havana's Vedado district! Unlike a standard BFS that expands outwards from a single source, Bidirectional BFS cleverly initiates two simultaneous searches: one from the starting point and another from the destination. Both searches utilize a **Queue** data structure to explore their respective neighborhoods level by level, prioritizing nodes closer to their origin. The real magic happens when these two expanding search frontiers meet. This intersection point allows us to construct the shortest path by combining the segments from both searches, effectively reducing the search space and, in many cases, significantly cutting down the time complexity.

Our "map" of Havana's Vedado is a tangible representation of **Graph Theory**. Each street intersection is a **node**, and the streets connecting them are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see pulsing outwards represent the edges being explored by our two BFS instances, a visual testament to their growing search radius. When these colored frontiers collide, the vibrant **yellow line** appears – that's our computed shortest path! Unlike a uniform grid where edge costs are often uniform, real-world road networks introduce complexities. Street layouts, one-way systems, and varying traffic conditions can influence edge weights. While this visualization simplifies those complexities for clarity, the fundamental advantage of Bidirectional BFS in pruning the search space remains powerful for optimizing pathfinding in such intricate, real-world graphs.
