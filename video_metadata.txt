**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find a route? Let's dive into the **Greedy Best-First Search** algorithm, visualized on a real map of Westlands, Nairobi! 🗺️ This algorithm is designed for speed, prioritizing exploration towards the target. At each step, it examines all unvisited neighbors of the current node and selects the one that is *closest* to the goal according to a heuristic function (in our case, straight-line distance). It doesn't look ahead to see if a slightly longer detour now might lead to a much shorter overall path later. Technically, it often utilizes a **priority queue** to efficiently manage and retrieve the neighbor with the lowest heuristic cost, allowing it to greedily "run straight towards the target."

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms the complex road network of Westlands into a graph. Each **intersection** acts as a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** represent the **edges**. The **red and green lines** you see illustrate the algorithm's exploration – the red edges signify visited nodes and paths it has considered, while the green lines show the path it has actively followed. The shining **yellow line** is the final computed path, representing the greedy decision-making process. Unlike explicit grid-based pathfinding where all moves have uniform costs, real-world road networks introduce complexities: varying street lengths, one-way systems, and traffic conditions (though our visualization simplifies this to focus on the core algorithm). This means the "best" neighbor in a real-world graph can be harder to determine and the greedy approach, while fast, might not always yield the globally optimal, shortest route.
