**Real Map Implementation of DFS** 🗺️

Ever wondered how algorithms like Depth-First Search (DFS) might navigate the labyrinthine streets of a city? This visualization brings it to life! DFS operates by exploring as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. It prioritizes a single, deep exploration path. When encountering a node with multiple unvisited neighbors, DFS arbitrarily chooses one to proceed down, committing to that direction until it hits a dead end or a previously visited node. This "go deep first" strategy is typically implemented using a **stack** (either explicitly or implicitly through recursion). Unlike algorithms that might explore breadth-wise (like BFS using a queue) or consider edge weights (like Dijkstra's using a priority queue), DFS's simplicity makes it very fast for certain problems, though it can get "lost" in vast or complex graphs if not carefully managed.

We've transformed the vibrant streets of Istanbul's Karaköy district into a graph! Each **intersection** is a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. In this visualization, the **red and green lines** represent the **visited edges** as our DFS algorithm diligently explores the graph, marking its traversal. The **solid yellow line** showcases the **computed path** that DFS ultimately determined. It's fascinating to see how this abstract concept translates to a real-world scenario. While a grid-based graph might have predictable branching, real-world road networks are far more dynamic. The complexity of finding paths on actual roads can differ significantly from theoretical grids due to factors like one-way streets, varying street lengths, and complex intersection layouts – elements that DFS can still effectively navigate, given the right graph representation.
