**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate complex networks? In this visualization, we're implementing Depth-First Search (DFS) to explore the streets of Seoul's Mapo-gu! 🗺️ DFS is a graph traversal algorithm that works by going as deep as possible along each branch before backtracking. It prioritizes exploring one neighbor completely before considering others. Think of it like choosing a street and following it until you hit a dead end or a previously visited intersection, then turning back and trying the next available street. Technically, DFS typically utilizes a **stack** (often implicitly through recursion) to keep track of the path being explored. When it encounters a node, it marks it as visited and then pushes its unvisited neighbors onto the stack. This LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) nature of the stack ensures we delve into the deepest parts of the graph first.

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms Mapo-gu into a graph where **intersections are nodes** and the **streets connecting them are edges**. The red and green lines you see in the video represent "visited edges" – the exploration process of DFS as it dives deep and backtracks. The bright yellow line showcases the **computed path** that DFS ultimately found to a destination. Unlike simple grid-based pathfinding where edge traversal is uniform, real-world road networks introduce complexities like one-way streets, varying street lengths, and traffic conditions, which can significantly influence traversal efficiency. While DFS is known for its speed in finding *a* path, its primary goal isn't necessarily the shortest path, making it suitable for tasks where reaching any valid destination quickly is paramount.
