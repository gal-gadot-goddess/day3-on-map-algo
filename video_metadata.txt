**Unpacking the Greedy Best-First Logic:**
Greedy Best-First search operates with a singular focus: minimizing an evaluation function that estimates the cost from the current node to the target. Unlike Dijkstra's algorithm, which considers the *total path cost* accumulated so far, Greedy Best-First only looks ahead. It prioritizes exploring neighbors that appear to be closest to the goal, often using a heuristic function (like Euclidean or Manhattan distance on a grid, or in our case, straight-line distance on the map). This "greedy" approach means it doesn't backtrack or reconsider previously visited paths if a seemingly better option appears. For managing its frontier of potential next steps, Greedy Best-First typically utilizes a **priority queue**. This data structure allows us to efficiently retrieve the node with the lowest heuristic evaluation at each step, driving the search directly towards the estimated target location.

**From City Streets to Graph Theory: Reykjavík's Vesturbær Revealed:**
In this visualization, we've translated the intricate street network of Reykjavík's Vesturbær into a **graph**. Each intersection is a **node**, and each street segment connecting two intersections is an **edge**. The **red and green lines** you see represent the **visited edges** as the algorithm explores the graph. Red signifies edges that have been evaluated and deemed less promising, while green indicates edges that are part of the current most promising path towards the target. The vibrant **yellow line** ultimately illustrates the computed path generated by the Greedy Best-First search. It's important to note that real-world road networks introduce complexities beyond a simple grid. Factors like one-way streets, turn restrictions, and varying road speeds mean that edge weights aren't uniform and the graph structure can be significantly more complex, potentially impacting the algorithm's efficiency and optimality compared to an abstract grid. While Greedy Best-First is often fast due to its focused exploration, its heuristic-driven nature means it's not guaranteed to find the globally optimal shortest path in these real-world, complex graph scenarios.
