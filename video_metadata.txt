**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find *a* route, even if not always the *absolute shortest*? Meet Greedy Best-First! This algorithm prioritizes exploring neighbors that are *closest* to the target, using a heuristic function (in our case, straight-line distance to Reykjavik's Miðborg). It's a single-minded explorer! Technically, it often uses a **priority queue** to manage its frontier. Each element in the queue is a node, ordered by its heuristic value. When a node is expanded, its neighbors are evaluated, and if not already visited, they are added to the priority queue. This greedy approach means it always picks the neighbor that *looks* most promising *right now*, without looking ahead to see if that choice might lead to a dead end or a longer overall path later.

This visualization brings graph theory to life in the charming streets of Reykjavik's Miðborg! We’ve transformed the city's intricate network into a graph where **nodes represent intersections** and **edges represent the streets connecting them**. The **red and green lines** you see are the "visited edges" – the algorithm is actively exploring different paths. The **vibrant yellow line** showcases the final path computed by Greedy Best-First. It's important to note that real-world road networks are far more complex than explicit grids often used for algorithm demonstrations. Factors like one-way streets, speed limits, and traffic conditions create a more dynamic and less predictable graph, which can significantly impact the efficiency and outcome of search algorithms compared to simpler, uniform cost grids.
