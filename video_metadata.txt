## Real Map Implementation of DFS 🗺️

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route? Dive with us into the fascinating world of **Depth-First Search (DFS)** as we visualize its application on the streets of Cape Town's City Bowl! 🇿🇦

DFS is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. Imagine you're exploring a maze. DFS picks a direction and keeps going until it hits a dead end or has explored all reachable paths from that point. It's inherently recursive or can be implemented iteratively using a **stack**. When visiting a node (an intersection, in our case), DFS pushes all its unvisited neighbors onto the stack. It then pops the top neighbor from the stack and recursively (or iteratively) explores from there. This "deep dive" approach prioritizes exploring one path completely before considering alternatives. Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which uses a **queue** to explore layer by layer, DFS's reliance on a **stack** naturally leads to a deeper exploration first.

In this visualization, we've transformed the intricate network of Cape Town's City Bowl into a graph! 🌃 Each **intersection** represents a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. The **red and green lines** you see aren't just pretty colors; they vividly illustrate the **visited edges** as DFS dynamically explores the graph. The **vibrant yellow line** highlights the **computed path** that DFS has successfully navigated through the cityscape. It's crucial to understand that applying DFS to a real-world road network presents unique challenges compared to a simple grid. Factors like one-way streets, varying street lengths, and complex intersection layouts add layers of complexity, impacting traversal efficiency and path computation. While DFS is generally fast for unweighted graphs, real-world traffic conditions and road network density can significantly influence its practical performance.
