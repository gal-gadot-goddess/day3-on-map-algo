**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wonder how navigation apps find *a* route, even if it's not always the *fastest*? Meet Greedy Best-First Search! This algorithm is all about prioritizing exploration based on a heuristic. In our visualization, it's like a curious explorer in the streets of SoHo, New York, always taking the next step that seems *closest* to the destination. It doesn't look ahead to see if a seemingly longer detour might lead to a much better overall path; it simply moves towards the target with each choice. Technically, Greedy Best-First typically utilizes a **priority queue**. This data structure is crucial because it allows us to efficiently extract the node (or intersection, in our case) with the lowest heuristic cost (estimated distance to the target) at each step. While a simple queue (FIFO) or stack (LIFO) would be used for Breadth-First or Depth-First respectively, the priority queue is what gives Greedy Best-First its focused, heuristic-driven approach.

This is where graph theory meets the real world! We've transformed the bustling streets of SoHo, New York, into a navigable graph. Each **intersection** is a **node**, and each **street segment** connecting them is an **edge**. The red and green lines you see represent the "visited edges" – the exploration paths our Greedy Best-First algorithm has traversed. The glowing yellow line? That's the computed path from our starting point to the target, determined by the algorithm's decisions. Unlike a simple grid where every step is predictable, real-world road networks have complexities like one-way streets, varying speeds, and connectivity nuances. This means the "edges" have implicit costs, and the heuristic we use (like Euclidean distance or Manhattan distance) is an approximation. Greedy Best-First, while often faster in finding *a* solution than exhaustive searches, can sometimes be tricked by local optima – situations where the closest immediate step leads away from the globally optimal path. This highlights the trade-off between speed and optimality in pathfinding!
