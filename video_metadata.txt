**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps *might* choose a route? Let's dive into the **Greedy Best-First Search algorithm**, illustrated here on the charming streets of Valparaíso's Cerro Concepción! 🗺️ This algorithm is all about making quick, optimistic decisions. It uses a **priority queue** to store its potential next moves. The key differentiator? It doesn't consider the cost *from the start* (like Dijkstra's), nor the total estimated cost to the goal (like A*). Instead, Greedy Best-First prioritizes nodes based *solely* on their **heuristic evaluation** – an educated guess about how close they are to the target destination. It greedily expands the node that *appears* closest to the goal, hoping to reach it swiftly. While this can be incredibly fast, this "myopic" approach means it can sometimes get stuck on suboptimal paths or even fail to find the shortest path if a seemingly distant node actually leads to a much better overall route.

Our Valparaíso visualization turns a real-world map into a **graph data structure**, a fundamental concept in **Graph Theory**. Here, each **intersection** represents a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see are **visited edges**, showcasing the algorithm's exploration as it traverses the graph, expanding outwards from the start. The bright yellow line? That's the **computed path** identified by Greedy Best-First. Implementing pathfinding on real-world roads presents unique challenges compared to explicit grid maps. Factors like **one-way streets, varying speeds, turn restrictions, and complex road networks** add layers of complexity that need to be modeled within the graph. This "real map implementation" highlights how abstract algorithms can be applied to tangible, intricate environments, demonstrating both their power and their limitations in practical scenarios.
