**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the fastest route? It's often thanks to clever graph traversal algorithms! This visualization showcases **Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)** in action on a real map of Tbilisi's Vake district. Instead of a single search from your starting point, Bidirectional BFS intelligently launches *two* simultaneous searches: one from the origin and another from the destination. Each search expands outwards layer by layer, prioritizing neighbors in a level-order fashion using **queues**. As soon as the two search frontiers meet, the shortest path is guaranteed to be found. This effectively halves the search space compared to a standard BFS, leading to significantly faster computation, especially in large, complex graphs.

Our "real map implementation" transforms the intricate street network of Tbilisi's Vake district into a digital graph. Each **intersection** acts as a **node**, and the **streets** connecting them are the **edges**. The animating red and green lines you see represent the "visited edges" as each search expands, exploring the graph's connectivity. The triumphant yellow line? That's the computed shortest path, forged at the crucial moment our bidirectional searches converged! Unlike the predictable, uniform nature of grid-based BFS, real-world road networks present unique challenges. Road lengths, one-way systems, and varying traffic conditions (though not explicitly modeled here) add layers of complexity that sophisticated algorithms like Bidirectional BFS are designed to navigate efficiently.
