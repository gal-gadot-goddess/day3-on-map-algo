**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps *try* to find the quickest route? Let's dive into the Greedy Best-First search algorithm! 🧠 This algorithm prioritizes exploration based on a heuristic function that estimates the cost from a given node to the target. In essence, it always expands the node that appears *closest* to the goal, aiming for rapid progress. It typically uses a priority queue to manage potential paths, ordering them by their heuristic cost. Unlike algorithms that guarantee optimality, Greedy Best-First makes locally optimal choices, hoping to reach the destination efficiently. This can be super fast, but it might lead you down a shortcut that ultimately takes longer if the heuristic is misleading, or if a slightly longer initial path leads to a significantly better overall route. Think of it as taking the most direct-looking sidewalk, even if it zigs and zags a bit, rather than meticulously planning every turn.

<br>

Our visualization brings this concept to life on a real-world map of Dublin, specifically focusing on the Smithfield area! 🇮🇪 To achieve this, we've transformed the actual street network into a graph. Each intersection in Smithfield becomes a node, and the streets connecting them are the edges. In the video, you'll see the algorithm in action: the red and green lines represent the edges (streets) that the algorithm has "visited" or considered during its search. The bright yellow line traces the path computed by Greedy Best-First as it navigates from start to finish. It's important to note that real-world road networks present unique challenges compared to simpler grid-based examples. Factors like one-way streets, varying speed limits, and complex intersections add layers of complexity to pathfinding that explicit grid algorithms might oversimplify. Greedy Best-First's approach, while not always the *absolute* shortest path, often provides a commendably fast and visually intuitive route in these dynamic environments.
