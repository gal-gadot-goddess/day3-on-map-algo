**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wonder how route-finding algorithms can be so efficient? Meet Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)! Instead of a single search radiating outwards from a starting node, Bidirectional BFS intelligently launches *two* simultaneous searches: one from the origin and another from the destination. Each search explores layer by layer, prioritizing immediate neighbors. To manage this, it typically utilizes two queues (one for each search) to store nodes at each level. As these expanding "waves" of exploration progress, they eventually meet. This convergence dramatically reduces the search space compared to a unidirectional BFS, effectively cutting the average search time by more than half! Think of it like two scouts meeting in the wilderness rather than one searching the entire territory alone.

This visualization brings Graph Theory to life in the vibrant streets of Salvador, Brazil (Barra!). We've transformed this real-world location into a graph where intersections are our **nodes** and the connecting streets are our **edges**. The red and green lines you see represent the expanding search frontiers of our bidirectional BFS, diligently exploring the city's road network. When these explorations collide, a path is found, highlighted by the bright yellow line. Unlike a perfect grid, real-world road networks are inherently more complex: they have varying edge weights (traffic, one-way streets, speed limits) and can be sparse or densely interconnected. While Bidirectional BFS offers a significant theoretical advantage on average, its practical efficiency can be influenced by the specific topology and characteristics of the graph it's navigating.
