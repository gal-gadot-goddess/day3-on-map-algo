**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional Dijkstra**

Ever wondered how your navigation app finds the quickest route? It often leverages sophisticated algorithms like **Bidirectional Dijkstra**. Unlike a standard Dijkstra that explores outward from a single source, this enhanced version initiates two simultaneous searches: one from the start node and another from the destination node. The key to its efficiency lies in how it prioritizes neighbors. Both searches maintain a priority queue, ordered by the cumulative weight (distance) from their respective starting points. Nodes are expanded based on the lowest cumulative weight, meaning the algorithm always explores the most promising paths first. When the frontiers of these two searches meet, the shortest path is found. This "meet-in-the-middle" approach significantly reduces the explored search space compared to a unidirectional search, especially in large graphs.

For this visualization, we've implemented Bidirectional Dijkstra on a real-world map of Lyon, specifically the La Croix-Rousse district. Here, graph theory comes alive: each **intersection** is a **node**, and each **street segment** connecting them is a **weighted edge** (representing travel time or distance). The red and green lines you see dynamically trace the "visited edges" as each of the two searches expands its reach across the city's network. The **yellow line** illuminating on the map represents the **computed shortest path** once the two search frontiers successfully intersect. Unlike explicit grid-based implementations where complexity might be more uniform, real-world road networks can be highly irregular. This means the density of nodes and edges, along with varying edge weights (traffic, speed limits), can significantly impact which search frontier expands faster and when the intersection occurs, showcasing the algorithm's adaptability.
