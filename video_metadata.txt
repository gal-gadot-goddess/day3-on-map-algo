**Real Map Implementation of DFS** 🗺️💡

Ever wondered how algorithms like Depth-First Search (DFS) can navigate the intricate web of a city? In this visualization, we're bringing DFS to life on the streets of Lisbon's charming Belém district!

At its core, DFS is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. Think of it like exploring a maze: you pick a path and go as deep as you can. If you hit a dead end, you retrace your steps to the last junction and try a different unexplored path. This "plunging deep" strategy is achieved by using a **stack** (implicitly or explicitly). When DFS encounters a node, it pushes it onto the stack and then picks an unvisited neighbor to explore, pushing that onto the stack as well. This LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) nature of the stack ensures we always explore the most recently discovered path first. Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which uses a queue to explore level by level, DFS prioritizes depth, making it potentially faster for finding a specific node or path, though it can sometimes get "lost" down long, winding branches if not carefully managed.

Here, we've transformed the real-world streets of Belém into a graph. Each **intersection** is a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. The **red and green lines** you see visualize the DFS algorithm in action: the red lines show the edges it's currently exploring, and the green indicates edges that have been fully explored from that particular branch. The **yellow line** represents the **computed path** found by DFS to reach its target. Implementing DFS on a real-world map presents unique challenges compared to a simple grid. Road networks are rarely uniform; they have varying degrees of connectivity, one-way streets (which introduce directed edges), and can be far more complex and sparse than a structured grid. This can lead to different performance characteristics and the need for more sophisticated graph representation and edge weighting if optimization becomes a factor.
