**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Navigating the urban maze of Gdansk (Wrzeszcz) with code! This visualization showcases Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS) in action. Instead of a single search originating from your starting point, Bidirectional BFS simultaneously launches two searches: one from the start node and another from the target node. These independent searches expand outwards layer by layer, effectively exploring the graph from both directions. At each step, the algorithm prioritizes exploring nodes at the current depth of *both* searches. This is typically achieved using two separate queues, one for each direction of the search. When a node visited by the forward search is encountered by the backward search (or vice versa), the algorithms have met, and a path has been found. This "meeting in the middle" dramatically reduces the search space and, consequently, the computation time compared to a standard unidirectional BFS, especially in dense or large graphs.

We've transformed the streets of Gdansk's Wrzeszcz district into a tangible graph for this demonstration. Each intersection represents a **node**, and each street segment connecting two intersections is an **edge**. The red and green lines you see are **visited edges**, illustrating the expanding exploration of the graph by our two BFS searches. The moment these explorations intersect, a path is computed – visualized by the bright yellow line. Unlike explicit grid-based pathfinding scenarios, real-world road networks introduce complexities. Street layouts are irregular, and edge weights (travel time or distance) can vary significantly. While Bidirectional BFS guarantees finding the shortest path in terms of the number of edges (or steps), adapting it for realistic road networks might involve using a Bidirectional Dijkstra's algorithm or similar weighted approaches to account for varying travel times on different streets, ensuring the *fastest* path, not just the shortest.
