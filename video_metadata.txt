## Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional A* 🗺️✨

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route through a maze of city streets? Meet Bidirectional A*, the "speed king" of pathfinding algorithms! Unlike a standard A* that searches outwards from a single start point, Bidirectional A* initiates *two* simultaneous searches: one from the origin and one from the destination. These intelligent searches race towards each other, effectively cutting down the search space significantly.

At its core, Bidirectional A* employs the familiar heuristic-driven exploration of A*. Both forward and backward searches maintain an "open set" (typically a **priority queue**) containing nodes that are candidates for exploration, ordered by their estimated total cost (f-score = g-score + h-score, where 'g' is the cost from the source and 'h' is the heuristic estimate to the goal). It also utilizes a "closed set" (often a **hash set or dictionary**) to keep track of visited nodes and avoid redundant processing. The algorithm intelligently prioritizes neighbors based on their f-score, aiming to expand the search frontier towards the most promising paths. When the two search frontiers meet (i.e., a node is in both the open sets of the forward and backward searches), a complete path has been found, often much faster than a unidirectional search.

Turning a real-world city like Lisbon's historic Alfama district into a computational graph is a fascinating application of Graph Theory. Each **intersection** in Alfama becomes a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. For this visualization, the **red and green lines** you see represent "visited edges" – the exploration frontiers of our forward (red) and backward (green) Bidirectional A* searches. They showcase how each search is systematically expanding its reach, exploring potential routes. The triumphant **yellow line** is the ultimately computed shortest path, discovered where the two search bubbles intersected. Unlike explicit grid-based pathfinding where every cell is a potential node, real-world road networks introduce complexities like varying edge weights (travel time or distance), one-way streets, and irregular connectivity, making algorithms like Bidirectional A* exceptionally valuable for their efficiency in navigating these intricate "graphs."
