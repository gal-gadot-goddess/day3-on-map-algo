**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps *quickly* suggest a route, even if it's not always the absolute *shortest*? Meet the Greedy Best-First Search algorithm visualized on a real-world map of Edinburgh's New Town! This algorithm embodies a "straight to the goal" mentality. At each step, it greedily selects the neighboring node that appears to be "closest" to the target destination based on a heuristic function (in this case, the straight-line Euclidean distance). It doesn't look ahead to see if a slightly longer intermediate step might unlock a much faster overall path, nor does it re-evaluate already visited paths like Dijkstra's. Instead, it uses a **priority queue** to store potential next nodes, always prioritizing the one with the lowest heuristic cost. This makes it incredibly fast for exploration, but it can sometimes get trapped in suboptimal routes.

Our visualization transforms the intricate street network of Edinburgh's New Town into a graph. Each **intersection** is a node, and each **street segment** connecting them is an edge. The red and green lines you see represent the **visited edges** as the algorithm explores the graph, progressively narrowing down possibilities. The shining **yellow line** highlights the path computed by Greedy Best-First from our starting point to our target. Implementing this on a real-world road network presents complexities beyond a simple grid; road restrictions (one-way streets, turn bans), varying speed limits, and unexpected dead ends all add layers of real-world constraints that a purely theoretical grid algorithm wouldn't inherently account for. This is the essence of applying graph theory algorithms to tangible, dynamic environments – a fascinating blend of algorithmic logic and practical reality!
