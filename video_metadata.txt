**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate complex environments? In this visualization, we're diving deep into the **Greedy Best-First Search algorithm** on a real-world map! 🗺️ The core logic of Greedy Best-First is its single-minded pursuit of the goal. Unlike algorithms that explore broadly, Greedy Best-First prioritizes expanding the node that *appears* closest to the target, based on a heuristic evaluation function (often Manhattan or Euclidean distance in grid-based scenarios, or a direct estimate here). It doesn't look ahead or consider the cost of the path already taken. Instead, it greedily chooses the next best move. Technically, while not strictly defined by a single data structure like BFS (Queue) or DFS (Stack), a common implementation uses a **priority queue** to efficiently retrieve the neighbor with the lowest heuristic cost.

This "Real Map Implementation" transforms the historic streets of Lviv's Plosha Rynok into a graph. Each **intersection acts as a node**, and the **streets connecting them are the edges**. The red and green lines you see visualize the algorithm's exploration: red indicates visited edges where the algorithm "looked" but didn't find a better path towards the target from that branch, while green represents edges that were considered and found to be promising. The final yellow line is the computed path, found by the algorithm's greedy approach. It's crucial to note that real-world road networks introduce complexities beyond simple grids. Factors like one-way streets, varying road lengths, and actual traffic conditions (which a basic heuristic wouldn't account for) can significantly impact pathfinding efficiency and optimality, often making greedy approaches less predictable than on idealized grids.
