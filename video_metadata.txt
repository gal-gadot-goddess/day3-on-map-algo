**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Dive into the world of pathfinding with a real-world application of the Greedy Best-First search algorithm! 📍🇪🇸 Visualized here on the intricate streets of Zaragoza's Casco Histórico, this algorithm tackles a fundamental graph theory problem: finding a route from point A to point B.

At its core, Greedy Best-First prioritizes exploring neighbors based on a heuristic function, aiming to move directly towards the target. Unlike algorithms that consider the total path cost (like Dijkstra's), Greedy Best-First is driven purely by the estimated distance to the goal from each potential next node. For this implementation, we're likely using a **priority queue** to manage the frontier of nodes to explore. Nodes with a lower heuristic value (i.e., closer to the target) are given higher priority, meaning the algorithm greedily expands the node it believes will lead to the destination fastest, without looking ahead at the cost incurred to reach that node. This makes it incredibly swift but, as we'll see, not always optimal!

Transforming a city like Zaragoza's historic center into a graph is a fascinating process. Each **intersection** acts as a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** represent the **edges**. In our visualization, the **red and green lines** trace the algorithm's exploration – showing which edges (streets) it has traversed and evaluated. The **bright yellow line** proudly displays the path computed by Greedy Best-First. You'll observe how this algorithm, designed for speed, can sometimes make seemingly counterintuitive turns. On real-world road networks, the complexity is amplified. Unlike simple grid-based pathfinding where edge costs are often uniform, real roads have varying lengths, one-way restrictions, traffic conditions, and even elevation changes, making heuristic selection and performance even more critical. The **time complexity** of Greedy Best-First is generally better than Dijkstra's on average (often closer to O(E) or O(V log V) with a good priority queue and heuristic), but its worst-case scenario can be significantly worse, potentially exploring the entire graph if the heuristic is misleading.
