**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route through a city? Let's dive into the fascinating world of graph algorithms with a real-world twist! In this visualization, we're exploring **Depth-First Search (DFS)**. The core logic of DFS is its relentless pursuit of depth. When faced with a choice of neighboring nodes (intersections, in this case), it arbitrarily selects one and continues traversing down that path as far as it can go. This "plunging deep" behavior means it prioritizes exploring one branch completely before it even considers exploring other potential branches. To manage this exploration, DFS typically utilizes a **stack** (either explicitly or implicitly through recursion). When it hits a dead end or a node it has already visited, it **backtracks** – effectively popping the last visited node from the stack and returning to a previous junction to explore an alternative route. Unlike algorithms that might use a **queue** (like Breadth-First Search) or a **priority queue**, DFS doesn't inherently consider the "cost" or "distance" of an edge; its primary directive is exploration.

This isn't just an abstract concept; it's a **Real Map Implementation**! We've transformed the charming streets of Reykjavík's Grandi district into a graph. Each **intersection** is a node, and each **street segment** connecting them is an edge. The red and green lines you see represent the edges being "visited" as the DFS algorithm explores the graph. The red lines show segments that have been traversed and are now part of the exploration history, while the green lines highlight the current path being investigated. The bright yellow line vividly depicts the **computed path** that DFS has identified. It's important to note that while DFS is often fast on explicit grids, its performance on real-world road networks can be more nuanced. Unlike a grid where each node has a limited number of neighbors, real roads can have complex junctions with many potential turns, significantly impacting the branching factor and the efficiency of the search.
