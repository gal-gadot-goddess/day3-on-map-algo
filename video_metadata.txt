**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate complex environments? In this visualization, we're diving into the **Greedy Best-First Search algorithm** applied to the charming streets of Valparaíso's Cerro Alegre, Chile! 🗺️ At its core, Greedy Best-First prioritizes exploring paths based on a heuristic function that estimates the *distance to the goal*. It doesn't look ahead to see the full consequences of its choices (unlike A*), but rather makes locally optimal decisions in each step. For managing its exploration, Greedy Best-First typically employs a **priority queue**. Nodes are inserted into the queue, and the algorithm always extracts and expands the node with the *lowest heuristic cost* to the target. This means it eagerly moves towards what it *thinks* is the best path, making it incredibly fast but, as you might see, not always the globally optimal solution.

Our "real map implementation" transforms the intricate urban landscape of Cerro Alegre into a **graph representation**. Each **intersection** in Valparaíso serves as a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. The vibrant red and green lines you see are "visited edges" showing the algorithm's exploration as it hops from intersection to intersection. The bright yellow line represents the path computed by Greedy Best-First. Unlike idealized grid-based graphs where edge costs are uniform, real-world roads present complexities: varying lengths, one-way streets, and traffic conditions. While Greedy Best-First can be incredibly efficient on simpler, explicit grids, its heuristic-driven approach on a real road network can lead to suboptimal routes because it doesn't consider the actual accumulated cost to reach a node. This highlights the trade-offs between speed and optimality in pathfinding.
