**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional A***

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the *fastest* route? Today, we're diving deep into **Bidirectional A***, a seriously efficient pathfinding algorithm often dubbed "the speed king"! Instead of a single search radiating outwards from the start node, Bidirectional A* intelligently launches *two* searches simultaneously: one from the starting point and another from the destination. These searches race towards each other, drastically reducing the search space.

At its core, A* uses a heuristic function to estimate the cost to reach the goal, combined with the actual cost from the start node. In Bidirectional A*, each search maintains its own open list (typically a **priority queue**) which stores nodes to be evaluated, prioritized by `f(n) = g(n) + h(n)`, where `g(n)` is the cost from its respective start node and `h(n)` is the heuristic estimate to the *other* search's start node. When the two search frontiers meet, a path is found. Neighbor selection in each search is guided by this priority queue, ensuring we explore the most promising nodes first, effectively pruning vast branches of the search tree. We'll typically use a **closed list** (often a hash set or dictionary) for each search to avoid revisiting nodes.

This visualization showcases a **real-world Graph Theory application** in the vibrant Gardens area of Cape Town. We've transformed the city into a graph where **intersections are nodes** and **streets are edges**. The red and green lines you see represent the **visited edges** by our forward and backward searches respectively, demonstrating how they explore the graph. The shimmering **yellow line** is the computed optimal path, found by connecting the meeting point of the two searches. Unlike explicit grids, real-world road networks are complex and irregular. The density of nodes and edges, one-way streets, and varying travel times (edge weights) present unique challenges and highlight the superior efficiency of Bidirectional A* on large, complex maps where it intelligently focuses its computational effort.
