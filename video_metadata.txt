**Real Map Implementation of DFS** 🏙️💡

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate the real world? Let's dive into a visual demonstration of Depth-First Search (DFS) using a map of Chicago's Lincoln Park! At its core, DFS explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. When faced with multiple unvisited neighbors (think street intersections), DFS arbitrarily picks one and dives in. This "go deep" strategy is often implemented using a **stack** (either explicitly or implicitly through recursion). Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which uses a queue to explore level by level, DFS prioritizes depth. This can be incredibly fast for finding *a* path, but it might not always be the shortest. The "getting lost easily" aspect comes from its inherent tendency to pursue lengthy, potentially suboptimal routes before considering alternatives.

This isn't your typical grid-based graph! Here, we've transformed the streets of Lincoln Park into a graph where **nodes represent intersections** and **edges represent the streets connecting them**. The red and green lines you see illustrate the DFS algorithm in action: red indicates explored edges, and green marks the path being actively traversed. The bright yellow line? That's the final computed path found by DFS. Implementing DFS on real-world road networks presents unique challenges compared to explicit grids. Factors like varying street lengths, one-way streets, and traffic can introduce complexities that don't exist in simpler graph structures. While DFS can quickly find *a* way through Chicago's urban maze, it might not always be the most efficient route in terms of distance or time, highlighting the trade-offs between different graph traversal algorithms in practical applications.
