**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route between two points? This visualization showcases a powerful graph traversal algorithm: Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)! Unlike a traditional BFS that starts from a single source and expands outwards, Bidirectional BFS initiates *two* simultaneous searches. One search originates from your starting location, while the other begins at your destination. These forward and backward explorations expand layer by layer, prioritizing neighbors that are closest in terms of edge count. The key data structure employed here is a **queue**, essential for the level-by-level expansion characteristic of BFS. Each search maintains its own set of visited nodes and potentially, its own queue. When the frontier of the forward search intersects with the frontier of the backward search, we've found our meeting point! This convergence dramatically reduces the search space, effectively cutting the exploration time in half compared to a single-directional BFS in many scenarios.

Our "real map implementation" brings this abstract algorithm to life within the charming streets of Prague, specifically the vibrant Žižkov district. We've modeled this urban landscape as a graph: each **intersection** represents a **node**, and the **streets** connecting them are the **edges**. The animation you see illustrates this beautifully. The **red** and **green lines** denote the **visited edges** as each search expands outwards, illuminating the path of exploration through the city's network. When these expanding wavefronts meet, the **yellow line** crystallizes into the **computed shortest path**, guiding you from point A to point B. It's important to note that while Bidirectional BFS excels on explicit grids, real-world road networks present unique challenges. Factors like one-way streets, varying road speeds, and traffic congestion introduce complexities that can influence pathfinding efficiency beyond simple edge counts, making algorithms like Dijkstra's or A* (which can incorporate edge weights) often more suitable for highly dynamic navigation systems.
