**Real Map Implementation of DFS 🗺️🕵️‍♂️**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route or how search engines explore the web? At its core, it often involves graph traversal algorithms like Depth-First Search (DFS). In this visualization, we're plunging into the labyrinthine streets of Lyon's La Guillotière district, transforming it into a digital graph to showcase DFS in action!

Depth-First Search is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. When it encounters a node (an intersection), it arbitrarily picks an unvisited neighbor and moves to it. This process continues recursively. DFS typically utilizes a **stack** data structure (implicitly through recursion or explicitly) to keep track of the path it's currently exploring. Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which uses a **queue** and guarantees finding the shortest path in unweighted graphs, DFS prioritizes *depth* over *breadth*. It doesn't use a priority queue as its traversal order isn't based on edge weights or priorities, but rather on the order in which neighbors are presented or explored. This can sometimes lead it down very long, unproductive paths, but its ability to explore deeply can be incredibly efficient for certain problems, like finding a path in a maze or detecting cycles in a graph.

Our "Real Map Implementation" takes the street network of Lyon's La Guillotière and models it as a graph. Each intersection is a **node**, and the streets connecting them are **edges**. In this visualization, the **red and green lines** represent the edges being actively explored by DFS – the algorithm is "visiting" these streets, attempting to reach its target. The **yellow line** signifies the final computed path found by DFS, demonstrating the sequence of streets traversed. While on an explicit grid, DFS can be quite efficient, navigating a real-world city introduces complexities. Road networks are rarely uniform grids; they have varying degrees of connectivity, one-way streets (directed edges), and potential dead ends, making the pathfinding more nuanced than a simple matrix traversal. The complexity of DFS in terms of time complexity is typically **O(V + E)**, where V is the number of vertices (intersections) and E is the number of edges (streets), which holds true for our real-world graph.
