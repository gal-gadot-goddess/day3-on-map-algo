**Real Map Implementation of Dijkstra**

Ever wondered how your GPS finds the *exact* shortest route, even through winding city streets? Enter Dijkstra's Algorithm, the gold standard for pathfinding. At its core, Dijkstra's is a greedy algorithm that meticulously explores a graph, prioritizing nodes based on their cumulative distance from a starting point. It maintains a set of visited nodes and distances to all other nodes. When selecting the next node to process, it always picks the unvisited node with the smallest known distance. Neighbors of this chosen node are then updated: if reaching a neighbor through the current node offers a shorter path than previously recorded, the neighbor's distance is updated, and it's added to a priority queue. This structured exploration ensures that the first time we reach the destination node, we've found its shortest path from the origin. The algorithm typically leverages a **priority queue** to efficiently retrieve the node with the minimum distance, along with a distance array (or map) and a predecessor array to reconstruct the path.

Transforming a real-world city like Lisbon's Alfama district into a graph for Dijkstra's involves mapping its urban fabric. Each **intersection** becomes a **node**, and each **street segment** connecting two intersections is an **edge**. The "weight" of each edge is crucial here: it represents the actual travel time or distance of that street segment, accounting for factors like speed limits, traffic patterns (if incorporated), and one-way streets. In our visualization, the **red and green lines** illustrate the **"visited edges"** – streets that Dijkstra's algorithm has explored, updating distances and pruning less optimal paths. The dynamic expansion of these lines shows the algorithm's systematic expansion from the starting point. Finally, the **shining yellow line** represents the **computed shortest path** to our destination. It's important to note that real-world road networks are far more complex than simple grids. They are often irregular, with varying edge weights and connectivity, making Dijkstra's ability to handle these complexities with its precise shortest-path calculation truly remarkable.
