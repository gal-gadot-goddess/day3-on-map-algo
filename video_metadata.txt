## Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First 🗺️✨

Ever wondered how navigation apps find *a* path (not always the *shortest*)? We're diving into the **Greedy Best-First Search** algorithm, visualized on a real-world map of San Francisco's SOMA district!

Greedy Best-First prioritizes exploration based on a heuristic function that estimates the cost from the current node to the goal. Unlike Dijkstra's, it doesn't consider the cost to *reach* the current node. In this implementation, we're using a **priority queue** to store potential next steps. Each element in the priority queue is a tuple: `(heuristic_cost, current_node)`. The algorithm repeatedly extracts the node with the lowest heuristic cost from the priority queue, marks it as visited, and then adds its unvisited neighbors to the queue, each with their own estimated cost to the target. This "greedy" approach means it always takes the step that *looks* most promising at that exact moment, aiming directly for the target without backtracking to explore potentially better, but currently less appealing, routes.

This isn't your typical grid! Here, we've transformed a section of San Francisco (SOMA) into a graph where **nodes represent intersections** and **edges represent the streets connecting them**. The red and green lines you see dynamically highlighting our exploration are the **visited edges**, showing the path the algorithm has traversed. The bright **yellow line** is the final path computed by Greedy Best-First from our starting point to the destination. Implementing this on real road networks introduces complexities beyond simple grids. Factors like one-way streets, varying street lengths, turn restrictions, and traffic conditions (which we haven't explicitly modeled here, but are critical in real-world routing) can significantly impact the performance and optimality of a greedy approach compared to a more exhaustive search.
