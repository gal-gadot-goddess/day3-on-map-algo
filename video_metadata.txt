**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wonder how navigation apps find the quickest route between two points on a complex network of roads? Today, we're visualizing a powerful algorithm: Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS). Instead of a single search expanding outwards from the starting point, Bidirectional BFS launches *two* independent BFS searches simultaneously – one from the start node and another from the target node. Both searches explore layer by layer, prioritizing neighbors based on their distance from their respective starting points. Primarily, a standard BFS utilizes a **Queue** data structure to maintain the order of nodes to visit, ensuring a level-by-level exploration. In this implementation, we're leveraging these queues to expand our search frontiers, intelligently avoiding unnecessary exploration of large portions of the graph.

This isn't just theoretical; we've brought Bidirectional BFS to life on a real map of Istanbul, specifically the vibrant Kadıköy district! Think of every intersection as a **node** and every street connecting them as an **edge** in a massive graph. The red and green lines you see dynamically tracing across the map represent the "visited edges" as our two BFS searches meticulously explore the graph from opposite directions. The bright yellow line? That's the magic – the shortest path the algorithm has computed by identifying where the two search frontiers meet in the middle. The efficiency gain is significant: by meeting in the middle, Bidirectional BFS can dramatically reduce the search space compared to a single unidirectional BFS, especially on large, non-uniform graphs like real-world road networks where not all paths are equally navigable. The complexity here differs from a simple grid; road networks have varying edge weights (travel time, distance) and connectivity, making these graph algorithms invaluable for practical applications.
