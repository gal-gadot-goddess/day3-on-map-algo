**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First 🗺️🚗💨**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find their way around? Today, we're visualizing the **Greedy Best-First** search algorithm on a real-world map of Belfast's Queens Quarter! 📍

This algorithm operates on the principle of "hopeful thinking" – it always prioritizes exploring the node that *appears* to be closest to the goal, based on a heuristic function. In our visualization, this heuristic is the straight-line distance (Euclidean distance) to our target destination. When considering which path to take next, Greedy Best-First doesn't look ahead multiple steps or consider the cost to reach the current node. Instead, it greedily picks the neighbor that minimizes the heuristic cost to the target. For data structures, it typically employs a **priority queue**, where nodes are ordered based on their heuristic evaluation, ensuring the most promising path is always explored first. While incredibly fast, this "straight-line approach" means it might miss a globally optimal path if it gets "stuck" on a locally optimal choice, potentially leading to suboptimal routes or even infinite loops in certain graph structures if not carefully managed with visited sets.

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms the streets of Belfast's Queens Quarter into a **graph data structure**. Each **intersection** is a **node**, and each **street segment** connecting two intersections is an **edge**. The red and green lines you see dynamically drawing on the map represent the **visited edges** of the graph as the algorithm explores. The red lines show edges that have been considered and are no longer the most promising, while the green lines highlight the current path being explored. The final **yellow line** is the computed path the Greedy Best-First algorithm determined. On explicit grids (like typical algorithm visualizations), edge traversal is uniform. However, on real roads, edge costs (travel time, distance) are highly variable and dependent on factors like one-way streets, traffic, and road curvature. Greedy Best-First, with its simple heuristic, can be computationally cheaper than algorithms like A* which consider both travel cost *and* heuristic, but it comes at the potential cost of optimality in complex, real-world road networks.
