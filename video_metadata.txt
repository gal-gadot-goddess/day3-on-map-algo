**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First** 🗺️✨

Ever wondered how navigation apps decide the quickest way from point A to point B? Today, we're visualizing the **Greedy Best-First** search algorithm in action, using the vibrant streets of Mexico City's Centro Histórico as our playground! 🇲🇽 This algorithm takes a direct approach: it always explores the path that *appears* to be the closest to the target, based on a heuristic. Unlike Dijkstra's algorithm which guarantees optimality by exploring the globally shortest path, Greedy Best-First prioritizes the neighbor node that seems most promising at each step. It typically uses a **Priority Queue** to store and retrieve the nodes to be explored, ordered by their heuristic cost (e.g., straight-line distance to the goal). This makes it incredibly fast for finding *a* path, though not necessarily the *absolute shortest* one.

In this "Real Map Implementation," we've transformed the intricate network of intersections and streets in Mexico City's Centro Histórico into a graph. Each intersection is a **node**, and each street segment connecting two intersections is an **edge**. The red and green lines you see represent the **visited edges** as the algorithm explores potential paths, expanding its search. The glowing **yellow line** is the final path computed by Greedy Best-First, a direct route chosen based on its heuristic evaluations. It's important to note that real-world road networks are far more complex than simple grids. Factors like one-way streets, traffic congestion, speed limits, and differing street widths create a much more intricate graph topology, where edge weights and heuristic accuracy can significantly impact the performance and optimality of search algorithms like this one.
