**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how algorithms find their way through complex mazes, or even cities? Today, we're visualizing the **Greedy Best-First Search** algorithm in action, navigating the charming, winding streets of Lisbon's Alfama district! 🇵🇹

Greedy Best-First Search is a pathfinding algorithm that prioritizes exploring nodes based on a heuristic evaluation of their distance to the target. Unlike algorithms that explore exhaustively, it makes a "greedy" choice at each step, selecting the neighbor that *appears* closest to the goal. This is typically implemented using a **priority queue**. The priority queue stores nodes to be visited, ordered by their heuristic cost (e.g., Euclidean or Manhattan distance to the goal). When the algorithm expands a node, it adds all its unvisited neighbors to the priority queue. The algorithm then extracts the node with the lowest heuristic cost from the queue and repeats the process until the target is found. While often very efficient, this heuristic-driven approach means it's not guaranteed to find the *shortest* possible path, as it might commit to a locally optimal choice that leads to a suboptimal global solution.

Our "real map implementation" transforms the intricate urban landscape of Alfama into a **graph**. Each intersection is represented as a **node**, and the streets connecting them are the **edges**. In this visualization, the red and green lines trace the **edges visited** by the algorithm as it explores potential routes. The red lines indicate edges that have been evaluated but not ultimately chosen for the final path, while green lines represent the exploration of promising branches. The final, yellow line showcases the computed path from our starting point to the destination. Implementing graph algorithms on real-world road networks presents unique complexities compared to simple grids. Road networks often feature one-way streets, varying speed limits (which can influence the "cost" of an edge), and non-uniform connectivity, making the "graph" representation and traversal more nuanced. Greedy Best-First shines here for its speed in finding *a* path, even if not always the absolute shortest.
