**Paragraph 1: The Logic of DFS**

DFS is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. Imagine it like exploring a maze: you go down one path until you hit a dead end, then you retrace your steps and try another. When prioritizing neighbors, DFS inherently uses a **Stack** (either explicitly or implicitly through recursion) to keep track of the nodes to visit. It picks the *most recently discovered* unvisited neighbor to explore next. Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which uses a Queue for level-by-level exploration, or algorithms using Priority Queues for weighted edges, DFS's stack-based approach makes it well-suited for finding a path or exploring all reachable nodes, though it can potentially explore very deep, unproductive branches first. This "getting lost easily" can be a drawback, but its simplicity and often faster discovery of *a* solution make it highly effective in many scenarios.

**Paragraph 2: Reykjavík's Streets as a Graph**

To bring DFS to life, we've transformed the real-world map of Reykjavík (specifically Vesturbær) into a graph. Each **intersection** acts as a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see represent the **visited edges** as DFS explores the network, marking paths it has traversed. The vibrant **yellow line** showcases the **computed path** found by the algorithm. Applying graph theory to real-world road networks introduces complexities beyond simple grid-based graphs. Factors like one-way streets, varying road lengths, and complex intersection geometries can influence traversal efficiency. While DFS is computationally efficient in terms of time complexity (O(V+E) for unweighted graphs), its performance on a real map can be affected by the underlying structure of the urban landscape. Unlike a perfectly uniform grid, the organic growth of a city means DFS might take longer to find an optimal or even a practical route if it gets caught in a convoluted network of side streets before finding the main thoroughfares.
