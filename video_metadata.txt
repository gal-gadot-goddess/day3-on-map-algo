**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate the real world? Here's a look at Depth-First Search (DFS) in action, visualized on the historic streets of Edinburgh's New Town! 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁳󠁣󠁴󠁿 At its core, DFS is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. It prioritizes going "deep" by selecting an arbitrary, unvisited neighbor of the current node and recursively visiting it. This "last-in, first-out" behavior is perfectly suited for a **stack** (either explicit or implicit via recursion) to keep track of the path taken. Unlike BFS (Breadth-First Search), which uses a queue to explore level by level, DFS's inherent nature can lead it down a lengthy, potentially "wrong" path before it realizes it needs to backtrack and explore alternatives. This can make it incredibly efficient for finding *a* path, but not necessarily the *shortest* path.

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms Edinburgh's New Town into a graph where **nodes** represent street intersections and **edges** are the streets connecting them. The visualization you see shows DFS at work: the **red and green lines** highlight the edges that have been "visited" by the algorithm as it explores, demonstrating its deep dives and backtracking. The **yellow line** traces the computed path it ultimately found. Applying graph algorithms to real-world road networks introduces complexities beyond simple grid-based scenarios. Factors like one-way streets, varying road lengths, and differing travel times (which would necessitate algorithms like Dijkstra's or A*) mean that a pure DFS might be faster in finding *any* route, but it doesn't guarantee optimality in terms of distance or time on complex urban landscapes.
