**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find routes? Let's dive into a visualization of the Greedy Best-First search algorithm applied to the vibrant streets of Lagos, Yaba! 📍 This algorithm is all about speed and efficiency, prioritizing exploration that seems *closest* to the goal at each step. It achieves this by maintaining a **priority queue** (often implemented with a min-heap) where each node (intersection) is evaluated based on a heuristic function that estimates its distance to the target. Unlike Dijkstra's algorithm, which guarantees the shortest path, Greedy Best-First is *myopic*. It greedily expands the node that appears to be the most promising neighbor, without considering the accumulated cost to reach that neighbor. This makes it incredibly fast, but it can sometimes lead to suboptimal or even dead-end paths if the heuristic isn't perfectly aligned with the true cost.

This visualization transforms the complex road network of Lagos (specifically Yaba) into a tangible graph! 🗺️ Each intersection is represented as a **node**, and the streets connecting them are **edges**. The red and green lines you see illustrate the **visited edges** – the algorithm's exploration process as it evaluates potential paths. The shining **yellow line** is the final path computed by Greedy Best-First from our starting point to the destination. It's crucial to understand that applying graph algorithms to real-world road networks presents unique challenges compared to simple grid-based examples. Road networks are often irregular, with varying edge weights (travel time, distance, traffic), one-way streets, and complex intersections, all of which add layers of complexity to pathfinding. The efficiency gains of Greedy Best-First are particularly appealing in such dynamic environments, but as the visualization shows, the path might not always be the absolute shortest!
