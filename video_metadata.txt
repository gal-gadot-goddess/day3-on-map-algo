**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Navigating the bustling streets of Cairo, Zamalek, we're visualizing the **Greedy Best-First Search (GBFS)** algorithm! GBFS is a fascinating pathfinding technique that prioritizes exploration based on a heuristic function – in essence, it *always* chooses the neighbor that *appears* closest to the target. Technically, it utilizes a **priority queue** to manage its frontier nodes. Unlike Dijkstra's algorithm which guarantees the shortest path, GBFS is purely "greedy." It doesn't look ahead at the total cost to reach a node from the start, only the estimated cost to reach the goal *from that node*. This can lead to faster exploration but sometimes results in suboptimal or even non-optimal paths, as it might commit to a seemingly promising route that turns out to be a dead end or a longer detour.

Our "city" is dynamically modeled as a graph where **intersections represent nodes**, and the **streets connecting them are edges**. The red and green lines you see trace the algorithm's exploration of the graph – the red lines show edges that have been considered but not yet expanded upon, while green indicates edges that have been visited and processed. The final yellow line is the path GBFS computed to reach our destination. Implementing graph algorithms on real-world maps like Cairo presents unique challenges compared to explicit grids. Real road networks have varying edge weights (travel times influenced by traffic, speed limits, one-way streets) and complex topological structures, making them far more dynamic and less predictable than a uniform grid. This makes GBFS's heuristic-driven approach both a compelling illustration of intelligent-like decision-making and a cautionary tale about the trade-offs between speed and optimality in complex, real-world scenarios.
