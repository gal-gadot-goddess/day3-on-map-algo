**Real Map Implementation of BFS**

Dive into the practical application of graph traversal with this visualization of Breadth-First Search (BFS) on a real-world map of Lisbon's historic Alfama district! 🇵🇹 At its core, BFS is a systematic algorithm for exploring all the vertices of a graph. It begins at a designated source node and expands outwards level by level, ensuring that all nodes at the current depth are visited before moving to nodes at the next depth. This "level-by-level" exploration is achieved by using a **Queue** data structure. When a node is visited, its unvisited neighbors are added to the back of the queue. The algorithm then dequeues the next node from the front of the queue and repeats the process. This prioritization of immediate neighbors guarantees that BFS finds the shortest path in terms of the number of edges in an unweighted graph, making it ideal for scenarios where efficiency in traversal distance is paramount. Unlike Depth-First Search (DFS) which utilizes a stack and explores deeply along one path, BFS is characterized by its breadth.

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms the intricate network of Lisbon's Alfama district into a computable graph. Each **intersection** in the city acts as a **node**, and the **streets connecting these intersections** are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see in the visualization represent the **visited edges** as BFS systematically explores the graph, radiating outwards from our starting point. The final yellow line highlights the computed shortest path from the origin to our destination. Implementing BFS on real-world road networks presents unique challenges compared to explicit grid graphs. While a grid is uniform, real roads have varying lengths, one-way restrictions, and complex junctions. Our visualization simplifies this by treating each street segment between intersections as a uniform "edge" to demonstrate the algorithmic principle. The time complexity of BFS on a graph with *V* vertices and *E* edges is typically O(V + E), meaning its performance scales linearly with the number of nodes and edges. On a dense urban map, this translates to a thorough, albeit potentially slower, exploration compared to a sparser or more structured grid.
