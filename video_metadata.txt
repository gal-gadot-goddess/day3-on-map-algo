**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate complex networks? Our latest visualization dives deep into Depth-First Search (DFS), a powerful graph traversal algorithm. DFS operates by exploring as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. When presented with a choice of neighbors to visit from a current node, DFS arbitrarily picks one and continues down that path. It doesn't consider edge weights or prioritize certain paths; it simply commits to one direction. This "go deep" strategy is typically implemented using a **stack** (explicitly or implicitly through recursion) to keep track of the nodes to visit and to facilitate backtracking when a dead end is reached. Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which uses a queue and explores level by level, DFS prioritizes depth.

This visualization brings DFS to life on the streets of Berlin's Mitte district! We've transformed real-world map data into a graph where intersections act as **nodes** and the streets connecting them are **edges**. The red and green lines you see represent the **visited edges** as DFS explores the network, progressively "discovering" the city's interconnectedness. The bright yellow line showcases the final computed path found by the algorithm. It's crucial to note that applying algorithms like DFS to real-world road networks introduces complexities not present in idealized grids. Road conditions, one-way streets, traffic, and varying edge lengths (street lengths) can significantly impact traversal time and path optimality, making direct comparisons to theoretical complexity on uniform grids a simplification of practical challenges.
