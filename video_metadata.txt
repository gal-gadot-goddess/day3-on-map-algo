**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find a quick route? Let's dive into the Greedy Best-First search algorithm, visualized on the charming streets of Valparaíso's Cerro Alegre! Unlike exhaustive search algorithms, Greedy Best-First prioritizes exploration based on a heuristic evaluation of each neighbor's proximity to the target. It doesn't look ahead to see if a seemingly optimal choice leads to a dead end later; instead, it greedily picks the neighbor that appears closest *right now*. Technically, it often utilizes a **priority queue** to manage the frontier of nodes to visit. Each node in the queue is prioritized based on its heuristic value (e.g., straight-line distance to the goal), ensuring the algorithm always expands the most promising node first. This focus on immediate gain makes it remarkably fast, but as we'll see, not always optimal.

Our "real map implementation" transforms the urban landscape of Valparaíso into a graph structure. Each **intersection** serves as a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see illustrate the algorithm's exploration process – tracing the "visited edges" as it probes different paths. The vibrant yellow line represents the final path computed by Greedy Best-First. Notice how on real-world road networks, the "edges" are not uniform like in a grid. Streets have varying lengths, one-way restrictions, and complex turns, which introduce nuances and potential inefficiencies that a simpler grid-based heuristic might overlook. This makes the performance and pathfinding characteristics of algorithms like Greedy Best-First particularly interesting to observe in such a dynamic, real-world context.
