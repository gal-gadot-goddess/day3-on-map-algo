**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find a quick route, even if it's not always the absolute shortest? This visualization showcases the **Greedy Best-First Search** algorithm in action, applied to the real-world streets of Tallinn's Kristiine district! At its core, Greedy Best-First is a **graph traversal algorithm** that prioritizes exploring nodes based on a heuristic function – essentially, how "close" a node *appears* to be to the target. It doesn't look ahead multiple steps; it makes the locally optimal choice at each stage. This means it's incredibly fast! To manage its exploration, Greedy Best-First typically utilizes a **priority queue**. Nodes are added to the queue, and the one with the lowest heuristic cost (closest to the target) is always extracted first for expansion. While efficient, this "greedy" approach can sometimes lead it down a path that seems promising initially but might bypass a slightly longer but ultimately better overall route.

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms the intricate network of streets and intersections in Kristiine into a **graph**. Each intersection acts as a **node**, and the streets connecting them are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see are the **visited edges**, representing the algorithm's exploration as it navigates the graph. The bright yellow line illustrates the **computed path** it ultimately determined. Unlike a simple grid where movement is restricted to cardinal directions, real-world road networks are far more complex. They have varying edge weights (travel times, distances), one-way streets, and complex interconnections. This complexity means that while Greedy Best-First is fast, its heuristic might not always account for these real-world nuances, highlighting the trade-off between speed and optimality in pathfinding.
