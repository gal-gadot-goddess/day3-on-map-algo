**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find a route? Let's dive into the **Greedy Best-First Search algorithm** in action, visualized on the vibrant streets of Lima's Barranco district! 🇵🇪 This algorithm is characterized by its aggressive pursuit of the goal. At each step, it evaluates the neighbors of the current node and **selects the one that appears closest to the target heuristic-wise**. Think of it as always taking the street that *looks* like it's leading you most directly towards your destination. Technically, Greedy Best-First uses a **priority queue** to manage its frontier of nodes to explore. The priority of each node is determined solely by its estimated cost (or heuristic distance) to the goal. This makes it incredibly fast because it prioritizes exploration that seems most promising, without considering the cost incurred to reach that node.

Our visualization brings this to life by transforming the **Barranco district into a graph**. Each **intersection** represents a **node**, and the **streets connecting them are the edges**. The red and green lines you see illustrate the algorithm's exploration – the red edges are streets it has considered but potentially not taken, while green signifies paths it has actively traversed. The bright yellow line is the **computed path** found by Greedy Best-First. Unlike the predictable grids often used in basic algorithm examples, real-world road networks are complex. They have varying street lengths, one-way systems, and irregular layouts. This means the "straight line" heuristic can be misleading, as a seemingly direct road might be significantly longer or impassable in reality. The complexity of Greedy Best-First on explicit grids is generally polynomial, but on real-world graphs, its performance can be more unpredictable due to the intricate nature of the connections and the accuracy of the heuristic.
