**Understanding the Depth-First Search (DFS) Logic:**

Depth-First Search is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. Think of it as venturing down a rabbit hole until you hit a dead end, then retracing your steps to try another path. DFS prioritizes exploring one neighbor completely before considering any others. Crucially, it utilizes a **Stack** (often implicitly through recursion) as its primary data structure. When DFS visits a node, it pushes that node onto the stack. Then, it examines an unvisited neighbor, pushes it onto the stack, and repeats the process. If it reaches a node with no unvisited neighbors, it pops the current node from the stack, effectively "backtracking" to the previous node to explore its remaining unvisited neighbors. This "plunge deep" strategy can be incredibly efficient for certain problems, but it also means DFS can get lost in very deep branches if not managed carefully.

**From City Streets to Graph Theory:**

Transforming Reykjavik's Miðborg into a usable graph is where the magic of Graph Theory comes alive! Each **intersection** acts as a **node** (or vertex) in our graph, and each **street segment** connecting two intersections is an **edge**. In this visualization, the **red and green lines** meticulously trace the **visited edges** as DFS explores the street network. The **green lines** highlight the path currently being investigated, while the **red lines** indicate edges that have been fully explored and backtracked from. The bright **yellow line** beautifully showcases the **computed path** found by DFS to a specific destination. It's important to note that real-world road networks introduce complexities not found in simple grids. Factors like one-way streets, varying traffic densities, and the sheer scale of connections mean that the performance and "gotchas" of DFS can differ significantly from its textbook implementations on explicit grid graphs. DFS's speed advantage often shines when the solution lies relatively deep within the graph, but its tendency to get lost can make it less ideal for finding the *shortest* path compared to algorithms like Breadth-First Search (BFS) in unweighted graphs.
