## Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First

Ever wondered how navigation apps find routes? This visualization showcases the **Greedy Best-First search algorithm** in action on a real-world map of Porto's Ribeira district! 🗺️

At its core, Greedy Best-First prioritizes exploring nodes that *appear* to be closest to the target, without looking ahead at the *overall* cost. It uses a **priority queue** to manage its "frontier" of nodes to explore. Each entry in the priority queue is a node paired with a heuristic value – in this case, the straight-line (Euclidean) distance to the goal. The algorithm repeatedly extracts the node with the smallest heuristic value from the priority queue and adds its unvisited neighbors to the queue. This greedy approach means it makes locally optimal choices, aiming to get to the destination as quickly as possible. It's computationally efficient for exploration but can sometimes lead to suboptimal paths if a seemingly closer path eventually hits a dead end or requires extensive detours.

In this **Real Map Implementation**, we've transformed the charming streets of Porto's Ribeira into a graph. Each intersection is a **node**, and the connecting streets are the **edges**. The **red lines** represent edges that the algorithm has visited and explored, marking its path through the graph. The **green lines** indicate edges that were considered but ultimately not part of the final path, showcasing the exploration process. The **bright yellow line** vividly illustrates the final path computed by Greedy Best-First. Unlike a perfect grid where edge weights are uniform, real-world road networks introduce significant complexities. Factors like one-way streets, varying speed limits, traffic conditions, and road curvatures mean that simply minimizing Euclidean distance might not always lead to the fastest or shortest *driving* route. This makes Greedy Best-First a fascinating, albeit sometimes imperfect, approach for real-world routing challenges.
