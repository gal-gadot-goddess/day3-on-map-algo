**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional A***

Witness the speed king in action! Our visualization showcases the **Bidirectional A\* algorithm**, a powerful pathfinding technique that truly shines in real-world applications like navigating the winding streets of Valparaíso's Cerro Alegre. Unlike its unidirectional counterpart, Bidirectional A\* launches two simultaneous searches: one moving forward from the start node and another backward from the goal node. Each search operates with its own priority queue, storing potential nodes to explore. The priority is determined by the classic A\* heuristic: the sum of the actual cost from the source (g-score) and the estimated cost to the goal (h-score). However, the crucial difference lies in how they choose their next move. The forward search prioritizes nodes that are close to the start and have a low estimated cost to the goal. Conversely, the backward search prioritizes nodes that are close to the goal and have a low estimated cost to the start. This dual-pronged approach dramatically reduces the search space, as the two search frontiers are much more likely to meet in the middle than a single search would be to reach the goal directly, especially in large or complex graphs.

Turning a vibrant city like Valparaíso into a computable graph is a fascinating application of **Graph Theory**. Each intersection is represented as a **node**, and the streets connecting them are the **edges**. The weight of each edge can represent the actual travel time, distance, or even traffic conditions on that street. In our visualization, the **red and green lines** are the "visited edges" – they illustrate the exploration undertaken by each of the Bidirectional A\* searches as they traverse the graph, expanding outwards from their respective starting points. The **yellow line**, our shining star, is the **computed path** – the most efficient route found when the two search frontiers successfully intersect. It's important to note that real-world road networks present unique complexities compared to simple grids. Factors like one-way streets, varying speed limits, complex intersections, and the sheer scale of the graph mean that Bidirectional A\*'s efficiency gains are not just theoretical but practically vital for rapid and accurate navigation.
