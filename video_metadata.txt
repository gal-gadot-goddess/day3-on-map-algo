**Real Map Implementation of DFS 🗺️🚶‍♂️**

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate complex spaces? Let's dive into Depth-First Search (DFS) and see it in action on the charming streets of Rome's Trastevere! DFS operates by exploring as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. When it encounters a node (an intersection), it prioritizes visiting one of its unvisited neighbors. This inherently recursive or stack-based approach means it dives deep into a path, potentially getting "lost" down a long route before it’s forced to retrace its steps and explore alternative branches. Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which uses a queue and explores level by level, DFS typically leverages a **stack** (either implicitly through recursion or explicitly) to manage the order of nodes to visit. This makes it exceptionally efficient for finding a path, though it doesn't guarantee the *shortest* path.

Our "Real Map Implementation" translates the intricate network of Trastevere into a graph. Each intersection is a **node**, and the streets connecting them are the **edges**. In this visualization, the **red and green lines** trace the visited edges as DFS explores the graph, showing how it systematically probes different routes. The **yellow line** represents the computed path found by the algorithm. Unlike a perfectly structured grid where distances are uniform, real-world road networks present varying edge weights (street lengths, traffic conditions, one-way systems) and dead ends, making graph traversal a fascinating challenge. DFS's ability to quickly plunge down a path makes it suitable for scenarios where finding *any* path is the priority, even if it's not the most direct.
