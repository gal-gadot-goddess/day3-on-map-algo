**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional A***

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the fastest route through a sprawling city like Bengaluru? Today, we're visualizing the **Bidirectional A* algorithm**, a true "speed king" in pathfinding! Unlike standard A*, which searches from a single start node, Bidirectional A* employs two simultaneous searches: one forward from the start and one backward from the goal. This dual exploration significantly prunes the search space.

At its core, A* (and by extension, Bidirectional A*) utilizes a **priority queue** to manage nodes that need to be explored. For each node, it calculates a heuristic value `h(n)` (an estimated cost from that node to the goal) and an actual cost `g(n)` (the cost from the start node to that node). The priority queue prioritizes nodes with the lowest `f(n) = g(n) + h(n)`. In our Bidirectional A* implementation, we maintain two separate priority queues, one for the forward search (using the heuristic `h_forward(n)`) and one for the backward search (using `h_backward(n)`). When the two search frontiers meet, the algorithm reconstructs the optimal path. The "smartness" lies in the heuristic function, which guides the searches towards each other, dramatically reducing the number of nodes examined.

This visualization brings **real-world Graph Theory** to life using the streets of Bengaluru's Indiranagar! We've transformed this urban landscape into a graph where intersections are our **nodes** and the streets connecting them are our **edges**. Each edge has a weight representing travel time or distance. The **red and green lines** you see represent the "visited edges" – the paths explored by our forward and backward searches respectively. The **shimmering yellow line** is the actual, optimized path found by the algorithm! Implementing this on real-world road networks presents unique challenges compared to simple grid-based pathfinding. Road networks are often irregular, with varying traffic conditions and one-way streets, making the heuristic estimations and graph representation more complex but ultimately more powerful.
