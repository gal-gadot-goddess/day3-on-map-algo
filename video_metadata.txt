**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Navigating the urban labyrinth of Singapore's Downtown Core has never been more visually compelling! In this visualization, we're showcasing **Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)**, a powerful graph traversal algorithm that dramatically optimizes shortest path calculations. Unlike a standard BFS that originates from a single source, Bidirectional BFS initiates two simultaneous searches: one from the starting node and another from the target node. Both searches expand outwards layer by layer, exploring neighboring nodes. The key to its efficiency lies in how it prioritizes exploration. Typically, a standard BFS uses a **Queue** data structure (FIFO - First-In, First-Out) to manage nodes to visit. Bidirectional BFS often employs two separate queues, one for each search direction. When a node is visited by one search, it's marked, and if it has already been visited by the *other* search, we've found our meeting point! This intersection signifies the shortest path. The algorithm intelligently avoids redundant explorations by checking against the visited set of the opposing search.

This isn't just theoretical graph theory; it's a **Real Map Implementation**! We've meticulously transformed Singapore's Downtown Core into a graph where **nodes represent intersections** and **edges represent the streets connecting them**. The vibrant **red and green lines** you see are the "visited edges," illustrating the simultaneous expansion of our two BFS searches across the city's road network. When these colored paths converge, the algorithm has successfully identified the optimal route. The elegant **yellow line** then highlights this computed shortest path, connecting your start to your destination. It's crucial to note that while BFS guarantees the shortest path on explicit grids with uniform edge weights (like moving one step in any direction costs the same), real-world road networks introduce complexities. Factors like varying street lengths, speed limits, and traffic conditions mean that even though Bidirectional BFS finds the path with the fewest *edges* (street segments), it might not always correspond to the *fastest* travel time without incorporating edge weights and more advanced algorithms like A*.
