**Real Map Implementation of DFS** 🗺️💡

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate the complex web of our cities? In this visualization, we're diving deep into **Depth-First Search (DFS)** using a real-world map of San Francisco's vibrant Mission District as our playground. DFS is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. It prioritizes exploring *one* neighbor fully before considering others. Think of it like a determined explorer who follows a single path until it hits a dead end, then turns back and tries another. This strategy typically relies on a **stack** (either explicitly managed or implicitly via recursion) to keep track of the nodes to visit. When a node is visited, its unvisited neighbors are pushed onto the stack, and the algorithm then pops the top element and continues the process. This "go deep first" approach makes it incredibly efficient for finding *a* path, though it might not always be the shortest.

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms the streets of the Mission District into a graph. Each **intersection** acts as a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see illustrate the **visited edges** as DFS explores the graph. The vibrant **yellow line** dynamically plots the **computed path** discovered by our algorithm. Unlike a simple grid, real-world road networks introduce complexities: varying street lengths, one-way streets, and the sheer scale of interconnectedness mean that the typical complexity analysis of DFS (often O(V+E) for explicit graphs) needs to consider these real-world nuances. While DFS can get "lost" in cycles or explore redundant paths in a large, complex graph, its ability to quickly descend into a promising branch is a powerful characteristic, especially when the goal is simply to find *any* valid connection.
