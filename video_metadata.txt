**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route so fast? This visualization showcases the magic of **Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)** in action on a real-world map of Porto's Miragaia district. Unlike a standard BFS that expands outwards from a single source, Bidirectional BFS cleverly initiates *two* simultaneous searches: one from your starting point and another from your destination. Both searches expand level by level, using **Queues** to manage the nodes to visit. At each step, the algorithm prioritizes exploring neighbors that are closer to the *opposite* search frontier, effectively "meeting in the middle." This dual-pronged attack significantly reduces the search space, especially in large or complex graphs, dramatically cutting down computation time compared to a unidirectional search.

Here, we've transformed the charming streets of Miragaia into a graph where **intersections are nodes** and **streets are edges**. The red and green lines you see represent the **visited edges** of our two BFS searches, showcasing how they've explored outwards from the origin and destination. The brilliant **yellow line** highlights the shortest path computed once the two search frontiers intersect. While the concept of graph traversal is straightforward on explicit grids, real-world road networks introduce complexities like varying edge weights (street lengths, traffic conditions, speed limits) which can necessitate modifications like Dijkstra's algorithm for truly optimal pathfinding. However, Bidirectional BFS still provides a powerful foundation for efficient exploration and route discovery in such environments.
