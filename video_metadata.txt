**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the fastest route? One powerful technique is **Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)**. Unlike a standard BFS that exhaustively explores from a single starting point, Bidirectional BFS initiates *two* searches simultaneously: one from the origin and another from the destination. Both searches expand outwards level by level, prioritizing neighbors by adding them to their respective **queues**. This parallel exploration allows the searches to converge in the middle, drastically reducing the overall search space and, consequently, the time complexity compared to a unidirectional BFS. The core principle is that the shortest path will always be found when the exploration frontiers from both ends meet.

Our visualization brings this algorithm to life on the streets of Auckland's Parnell. We've transformed the urban landscape into a graph where **intersections act as nodes** and **streets as edges**. The red and green lines you see represent the edges being "visited" by each respective BFS search as it expands. When these two exploration frontiers collide, the algorithm has found its meeting point. The triumphant yellow line then depicts the computed shortest path between your starting point and destination. Interestingly, applying graph theory to real-world road networks presents unique challenges. Unlike a uniform grid where edge costs are equal, real roads have varying lengths, traffic conditions, and speed limits. This means that while Bidirectional BFS still offers significant performance gains, a more advanced variant like **Bidirectional Dijkstra's Algorithm** might be employed in production for pathfinding on complex real-world graphs to account for these edge weights.
