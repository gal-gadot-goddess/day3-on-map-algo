**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route? Beyond simple Dijkstra, many employ sophisticated graph traversal algorithms. Today, we're visualizing **Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)** in action! Unlike a standard BFS that expands outwards from a single source, Bidirectional BFS initiates *two* searches simultaneously: one from your starting point and another from your destination. Both searches utilize a **Queue** data structure to systematically explore reachable nodes layer by layer. The key to its efficiency lies in how it prioritizes checking visited nodes: each search keeps track of which nodes have been visited by the *other* search. When the two search frontiers intersect, a path is found, and we've effectively "met in the middle." This dual-ended exploration significantly reduces the search space, often cutting down computation time compared to a unidirectional search, especially in large graphs.

This visualization leverages real-world map data of Reykjavík's Grandi district to demonstrate graph theory principles. We've transformed the city's road network into a graph where **intersections act as nodes** and **streets connecting them are edges**. The red and green lines you see represent the **"visited edges"** being explored by our two BFS instances. They are expanding outwards, systematically uncovering the network. The golden yellow line illustrates the **computed path** that emerges precisely when the two searches converge. While this visualization depicts a simplified graph, it's important to note that real-world road networks are far more complex than an explicit grid. Factors like one-way streets, traffic lights, and varying travel times introduce further complexities that more advanced algorithms or heuristics (like A*) might address for optimal real-time navigation.
