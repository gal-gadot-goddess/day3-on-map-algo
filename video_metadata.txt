## Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional Dijkstra 🗺️✨

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route through a sprawling city? This visualization showcases **Bidirectional Dijkstra**, a sophisticated algorithm that turns real-world maps into a playground for graph theory! Unlike a standard Dijkstra, Bidirectional Dijkstra operates with two simultaneous searches. One starts from your origin, expanding outwards, while the other simultaneously begins from your destination, searching backwards.

At its core, both searches prioritize exploring the "closest" nodes first. This is achieved using a **priority queue**, which efficiently stores and retrieves nodes based on their accumulated path cost. When a node is processed, its neighbors are considered. For each neighbor, the algorithm calculates the tentative distance from the starting node (or to the destination node, for the backward search) and updates its entry in the priority queue if a shorter path is found. The searches continue until they "meet in the middle" – a node that has been visited by both forward and backward searches. This meeting point signifies the guaranteed shortest path between the origin and destination. This elegant dual-search strategy significantly reduces the explored search space compared to a unidirectional approach, making it the ultimate optimizer for finding shortest paths.

This video demonstrates a **Real Map Implementation** of Bidirectional Dijkstra, using the bustling streets of Tbilisi's Vake Park as our graph! In this context, **nodes** represent actual intersections in the city, and **edges** are the streets connecting them. The **red and green lines** you see are the "visited edges" – the areas of the graph actively being explored by our forward and backward Dijkstra searches, respectively. Notice how they strategically expand, prioritizing the most promising paths. The **bright yellow line** is the beautifully computed shortest path, elegantly connecting your starting point to your destination. It's crucial to understand that applying this to real-world road networks often presents more complexity than explicit grid-based graphs. Road networks are inherently irregular, with varying edge weights (traffic, speed limits, one-way streets) and a less predictable structure, making the efficiency gains of Bidirectional Dijkstra even more pronounced in finding that optimal route.
