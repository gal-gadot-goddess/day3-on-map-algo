**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps efficiently find the fastest route between two points? Today, we're diving into a powerful algorithm: Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)! Unlike a standard BFS that starts from a single source and expands outwards, Bidirectional BFS intelligently launches *two* searches simultaneously. One search originates from our starting point, and the other begins from our destination. Both searches expand layer by layer, exploring neighbors, until they eventually meet. This convergence in the "middle" drastically reduces the search space, often cutting the time complexity in half compared to a unidirectional approach, especially in large graphs. At its core, Bidirectional BFS typically utilizes queues (one for each search) to maintain the order of nodes to visit, ensuring a level-by-level exploration.

This visualization brings Bidirectional BFS to life on a real-world map of Lagos, specifically Yaba! We've transformed the intricate network of streets and intersections into a graph where intersections are our **nodes** and the streets connecting them are our **edges**. Observe the vibrant red and green lines: these represent the edges being explored by our two simultaneous BFS searches, each diligently expanding its reach. When these exploration frontiers collide, the algorithm reconstructs the shortest path, elegantly highlighted by the yellow line. It's crucial to note that real-world road networks, with their varying street lengths and complex interconnections, present a far more challenging and dynamic graph than simple explicit grids. This implementation showcases the practical power of graph theory in solving complex spatial problems.
