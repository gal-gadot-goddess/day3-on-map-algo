**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

 Ever wondered how pathfinding algorithms navigate complex environments? In this visualization, we demonstrate the **Greedy Best-First search algorithm** in action, applied to the vibrant streets of Wellington's Te Aro district! 🗺️ Algorithmically, Greedy Best-First operates by prioritizing nodes for exploration based on a heuristic function that estimates the cost to reach the goal. Unlike algorithms like A* which consider both the cost incurred so far (g-score) and the estimated future cost (h-score), Greedy Best-First *only* considers the estimated cost to reach the goal (h-score). This means it always chooses the neighbor that *appears closest* to the target, without regard for how many steps it took to get there. This heuristic-driven approach often leads to a rapid descent towards the solution, making it exceptionally *fast* in many scenarios. Technically, it typically utilizes a **priority queue** to efficiently manage and retrieve the node with the lowest heuristic value at each step.

Our "Real Map Implementation" transforms the intricate road network of Te Aro into a **graph**. Here, each **intersection** represents a **node**, and the **streets connecting them** are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see visualize the algorithm's exploration process. Red lines indicate edges that have been considered and potentially discarded at a given step, while green lines represent edges that are currently being explored or deemed promising. The vibrant yellow line ultimately showcases the path computed by Greedy Best-First. It's crucial to note that the complexity and performance of such algorithms can differ significantly in real-world road networks compared to abstract grids. Real roads often have varying lengths, one-way restrictions, traffic patterns, and complex intersections, introducing nuances that simple grid-based implementations might not fully capture. While Greedy Best-First's focus on speed makes it appealing, its reliance solely on the heuristic means it can sometimes be "short-sighted" and miss the globally optimal or shortest path, a trade-off to consider in critical applications.
