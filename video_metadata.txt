**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

Dive into the fascinating world of algorithms with this real-world visualization of Depth-First Search (DFS)! 🗺️ Our DFS algorithm plunges deep into a path, exploring as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. Think of it like a curious explorer systematically navigating a labyrinth. When encountering a node (an intersection in our city), DFS prioritizes exploring one of its unvisited neighbors before considering others. This "go deep first" strategy is fundamentally managed using a **stack** data structure (often implicitly via recursion, or explicitly). Unlike algorithms that might use a queue (like BFS for shortest path) or a priority queue for weighted edges, DFS's stack-driven nature means it's constantly pushing new exploration frontiers onto its "to-do" list and popping them off to investigate. While it can sometimes get "lost" and explore a long, non-optimal path before finding a solution, its recursive nature and efficient use of a stack can make it remarkably fast for certain problems.

This isn't just an abstract grid; we're bringing graph theory to life in the vibrant streets of Reykjavík's Miðborg! 🇮🇸 To create this real-world graph, each **intersection** acts as a **node**, and each **street segment** connecting them is an **edge**. The red and green lines you see tracing the city are our "visited edges," representing the DFS's exploration as it traverses the graph. The bright **yellow line** shows the computed path from a starting point to a destination – a testament to DFS's ability to find *a* path. It's crucial to note the complexity differences here compared to explicit grid graphs. Real-world road networks are often sparser, with varying edge weights (travel time, distance), and can have complex topologies. While DFS is generally O(V + E) where V is vertices and E is edges, the efficiency and practical pathfinding on a real map can be influenced by factors like one-way streets, traffic, and the inherent structure of the urban sprawl, making this visualization a powerful demonstration of algorithmic application beyond theoretical constructs.
