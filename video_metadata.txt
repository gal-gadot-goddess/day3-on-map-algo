**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

Ever wondered how algorithms like Depth-First Search (DFS) could navigate something as complex as a city? Our latest visualization brings this to life! DFS is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. Its core logic involves picking an unvisited neighbor and recursively visiting its neighbors. If it reaches a dead end or a fully explored path, it backtracks to the nearest node with unvisited neighbors. For this, DFS typically utilizes a **stack** (either implicitly through recursion or explicitly) to keep track of the path currently being explored. Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which prioritizes exploring all immediate neighbors (often using a queue), DFS dives deep, prioritizing depth over breadth.

This visualization uses the charming labyrinth of Lisbon's Alfama district as our real-world graph. Each intersection becomes a **node**, and the streets connecting them are the **edges**. The red and green lines you see represent the **visited edges** as DFS explores the graph, meticulously marking where it has been. The bright yellow line illustrates the **computed path** found by the algorithm. Implementing DFS on a real-world map presents unique challenges compared to simple grids. Real road networks have varying edge weights (travel times, distances), which can make a naive DFS, which doesn't consider edge costs, inefficient or lead to suboptimal paths. While DFS itself has a time complexity of O(V + E) where V is the number of vertices and E is the number of edges, its practical performance in a city can be affected by factors not explicitly encoded in a basic graph, like one-way streets, traffic, and accessibility.
