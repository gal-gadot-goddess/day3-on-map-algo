**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First**

Ever wondered how pathfinding algorithms work in the real world? Our latest visualization demonstrates the **Greedy Best-First search algorithm** in action, applied to the charming streets of Battery Point, Hobart.

At its core, Greedy Best-First prioritizes exploring nodes that appear to be closest to the target. Unlike algorithms like Dijkstra's which guarantee the shortest path, Greedy Best-First is a *heuristic* search. It greedily chooses the next node to explore based solely on an evaluation function that estimates the cost from the current node to the goal. This makes it remarkably fast. Technically, it typically uses a **priority queue** to manage its open set of nodes to visit. The priority is determined by the heuristic function, often the Euclidean or Manhattan distance to the target. When selecting the next node, it simply extracts the node with the lowest heuristic value from the priority queue. While this direct approach can lead to swift discovery of a path, it may not always be the *optimal* path, as it doesn't consider the cost already incurred to reach the current node.

This video translates the abstract concept of graph theory into a tangible, real-world scenario. We've modeled Battery Point, Hobart, as a graph: each **intersection** is a **node**, and each **street segment** connecting two intersections is an **edge**. The **red and green lines** you see visualize the edges being explored by the algorithm as it navigates the graph. The **yellow line** represents the path found by Greedy Best-First from the starting point to our designated target. It's important to note that while grid-based pathfinding often deals with uniform edge costs, real-world roads have complexities like one-way streets, varying lengths, and traffic conditions (though our simplified model doesn't account for the latter). This difference significantly impacts the performance and complexity of pathfinding algorithms when applied to actual urban environments compared to theoretical grids.
