**Real Map Implementation of DFS**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route through a maze of streets? We're diving deep into **Depth-First Search (DFS)**, a fundamental graph traversal algorithm, visualized right here on the charming streets of Lisbon's Alfama district! 🗺️ In DFS, we explore as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. When we encounter an intersection (node), we prioritize visiting one of its unvisited neighbors. This "going deep" strategy is achieved using a **stack** (explicitly or implicitly through recursion), which perfectly mimics the "last-in, first-out" nature of plunging down a path. Unlike algorithms that might use a queue (like Breadth-First Search) or a priority queue, DFS doesn't inherently prioritize shorter paths; it just goes deep! This can sometimes lead it down long, winding alleys, but its simplicity and potential for speed are undeniable.

Our "Real Map Implementation" translates the labyrinthine alleys of Alfama into a graph! Each street intersection becomes a **node**, and the streets connecting them are **edges**. The mesmerizing red and green lines you see aren't just pretty colors; they represent the **visited edges** as our DFS algorithm explores the graph. Red signifies an edge that has been explored and potentially backtracked from, while green highlights the active exploration path. The bright yellow line? That's the final computed path found by the DFS, guiding us through the ancient district. It's crucial to note that real-world road networks are far more complex than simple grids. Factors like one-way streets, traffic conditions, and road curvatures mean that the theoretical time complexity of DFS (O(V+E), where V is vertices and E is edges) can be heavily influenced by the dynamic nature of the graph, making real-world pathfinding a fascinating challenge!
