**Real Map Implementation of DFS 🗺️**

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate complex networks? Today, we're visualizing Depth-First Search (DFS) in action on the streets of Oslo's Sagene district! DFS is a graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. When presented with multiple unvisited neighbors of a node, DFS arbitrarily picks one and continues its descent. It's inherently recursive (or can be implemented iteratively using a stack) and prioritizes depth over breadth. Unlike Breadth-First Search (BFS) which uses a queue to explore level by level, DFS typically leverages a stack (explicitly or implicitly through recursion) to keep track of the nodes to visit. This "dive deep" strategy can make it incredibly efficient for finding *a* path, but also means it might not always find the *shortest* path.

This visualization transforms the real-world street layout of Sagene, Oslo, into a graph. Each intersection becomes a 'node', and the streets connecting them are the 'edges'. The pulsating red and green lines you see represent 'visited edges' – the algorithm's journey as it explores possible paths. The bright yellow line traces the final computed path found by our DFS implementation. It's fascinating to see how this abstract algorithmic concept applies to tangible urban planning! It's important to note that real-world road networks present unique complexities compared to simple grid graphs; factors like one-way streets, road closures, and varying traffic conditions significantly impact traversal efficiency and the practicality of direct DFS applications without additional heuristics.
