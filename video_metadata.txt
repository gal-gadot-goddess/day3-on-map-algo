**Real Map Implementation of Bidirectional BFS 🗺️**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest route so fast? We're diving into a powerful search algorithm: Bidirectional Breadth-First Search (BFS)! Unlike a standard BFS that explores outward from a single source, Bidirectional BFS initiates two simultaneous searches – one from the starting point (our origin in Dublin's Docklands) and another from the destination (our target intersection). Both searches expand layer by layer, exploring neighboring nodes (intersections) and edges (streets) in parallel. At each step, we typically prioritize exploring nodes that are closer to their respective starting points, often using a standard Queue data structure for both forward and backward searches. The magic happens when these two expanding frontiers eventually "meet" in the middle. This intersection point is crucial because it signifies that a path has been found from both the start and the end.

This visualization brings graph theory to life on the streets of Dublin's Docklands! We've transformed this urban landscape into a graph where intersections act as our nodes and the connecting streets are our edges. The red and green lines you see represent the "visited edges" as our two BFS searches meticulously explore the network. They highlight the expanding frontiers, showing how the algorithm systematically discovers reachable parts of the graph from both directions. The vibrant yellow line? That's the computed shortest path, cleverly constructed by connecting the meeting point of the two searches. While a standard BFS on an explicit grid might have predictable complexity, real-world road networks introduce nuances. Factors like one-way streets, varying speed limits, and the sheer density of connections can influence the actual performance and the perceived "meeting point" of our searches. This approach effectively halves the search space compared to a unidirectional BFS in many scenarios, leading to significantly faster pathfinding.
