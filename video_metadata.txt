## Real Map Implementation of DFS 🗺️

Ever wondered how algorithms navigate the intricate pathways of our world? Today, we're diving deep into a **Depth-First Search (DFS)** implementation using the beautiful streets of Stockholm's Södermalm as our real-world graph!

**Paragraph 1: The DFS Dive**

Depth-First Search operates on the principle of **exploring as far as possible along each branch before backtracking.** When faced with multiple unvisited neighbors (representing connecting streets in our map), DFS arbitrarily picks one and continues its descent. This "go deep" strategy is typically managed using a **stack** (either implicitly through recursion or explicitly with a `std::stack` or `Stack` data structure). Unlike Breadth-First Search which uses a queue to explore level by level, DFS prioritizes depth. This means it can sometimes take a "long way around" or get "lost" in a particular branch if it's not the most direct route to the target. However, for certain problems, like finding *any* path or exploring connected components, this focused exploration can be incredibly efficient in terms of time complexity, especially when the graph is sparse.

**Paragraph 2: Södermalm as a Graph**

Transforming a real city like Stockholm's Södermalm into a computational graph is a fascinating application of Graph Theory. We define **nodes** as key **intersections** and **edges** as the **streets** connecting them. In this visualization, the **red and green lines** dynamically trace the **visited edges** as our DFS algorithm actively explores the network, attempting to find a path. The **yellow line** highlights the **computed path** once the algorithm has successfully navigated from our starting point to the destination. It's crucial to note the complexity differences here compared to a simple grid. Real-world road networks are often much more complex, with varying edge weights (traffic, one-way streets), irregular structures, and potentially dead ends that can make explicit grid-based DFS implementations seem trivial by comparison. This demonstrates the power of abstracting real-world problems into graph structures for algorithmic analysis and problem-solving.
