**Real Map Implementation of DFS** 📍 Vancouver, Kitsilano 🗺️

Ever wondered how algorithms tackle complex navigation? This visualization dives deep into **Depth-First Search (DFS)**, an exploration strategy that embodies the spirit of "going deep before going wide." In DFS, we prioritize plunging into one branch of the search space as far as possible before backtracking. Think of it like exploring a maze: you follow one path until you hit a dead end, then retrace your steps to the last junction and try a different path. Technically, DFS typically uses a **stack** (either implicitly through recursion or explicitly) to manage the order of exploration. When visiting a node, we push its unvisited neighbors onto the stack. The algorithm then pops the next node from the stack and repeats the process. This inherently prioritizes deeper exploration, as newly discovered neighbors are immediately added to the top of the stack, becoming the next candidates for exploration. Unlike algorithms that might consider edge weights or distance (like Dijkstra's or A*), DFS is purely about systematic exploration of depth.

This isn't just abstract code; it's a **Real Map Implementation!** We've translated the urban landscape of Vancouver's Kitsilano neighborhood into a **graph**. Each **intersection** is a **node**, and the **streets connecting them are edges**. The vibrant **red and green lines** you see illustrate the "visited edges" as DFS actively explores the graph, meticulously charting out every street it traverses. The **shimmering yellow line** represents the **computed path** – the route identified by DFS after its exploration. It's fascinating to see how DFS, while potentially getting "lost" in cycles or suboptimal paths on a real-world road network (which is far less structured than a simple grid), can still efficiently find a route. The complexity of navigating real roads, with one-way streets, varying road lengths, and potential dead ends, offers a richer, more nuanced challenge compared to the explicit grid graphs often used for basic algorithm demonstrations. DFS, despite its potential for "getting lost," can be surprisingly fast at finding *a* path, especially in dense networks.
