**Real Map Implementation of Greedy Best-First 🗺️✨**

Ever wondered how navigation apps find the quickest way? Let's dive into the Greedy Best-First search algorithm, visualized on the vibrant streets of San Francisco's Mission District! 🌆

At its core, Greedy Best-First operates by greedily expanding the node that appears closest to the target. It doesn't look ahead to see if a seemingly good move might lead to a dead end; it simply prioritizes immediate proximity. This strategy typically employs a **priority queue** (or sometimes a min-heap) to store and efficiently retrieve the node with the lowest heuristic cost – the estimated distance to the goal. Unlike algorithms that explore uniformly (like Breadth-First Search with a standard queue), Greedy Best-First is driven by an evaluation function, often a simple heuristic like the Euclidean or Manhattan distance, aiming to reach the destination with minimal steps *as perceived by the heuristic*.

Transforming a real-world city like San Francisco's Mission District into a navigable graph involves abstracting its intricate network. Each **intersection** becomes a **node**, and each **street segment** connecting two intersections is an **edge**. In our visualization, the red and green lines represent **visited edges**, illustrating the algorithm's exploration path as it evaluates potential routes. The bright yellow line showcases the **computed path** – the sequence of streets it ultimately determines. It's crucial to note that navigating real roads presents unique complexities compared to idealized grid maps. Factors like one-way streets, turn restrictions, varying speed limits, and traffic congestion all influence edge weights and connectivity, making the "cost" of traversing an edge a dynamic and multifaceted calculation. While Greedy Best-First is swift, its reliance on a heuristic means it might not always find the absolute shortest path if the heuristic is misleading, a common challenge in complex, real-world pathfinding.
