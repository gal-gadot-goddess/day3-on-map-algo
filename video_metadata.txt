**Paragraph 1: Demystifying DFS Logic**

Depth-First Search is a fundamental graph traversal algorithm that explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. Its core mechanism involves prioritizing **unvisited neighbors** of the current node. When presented with multiple options, DFS doesn't pick the "closest" or "best" in a predefined sense; it simply dives deep into the *first* unvisited neighbor it encounters. This inherent "go deep" strategy is typically implemented using a **stack** (either explicitly or implicitly via recursion). As it explores a path, it pushes nodes onto the stack. If it hits a dead end or a previously visited node, it pops the last node from the stack, effectively backtracking to explore another branch. This differs significantly from Breadth-First Search (BFS), which uses a queue to explore layer by layer. The "lost easily" aspect arises because DFS can get stuck exploring a very deep, unproductive path, but its advantage lies in its potential speed and lower memory footprint in certain graph structures compared to BFS when a solution is found quickly.

**Paragraph 2: Miraflores as a Graph & Visualizing the Journey**

Transforming a bustling city like Miraflores into a graph involves treating **intersections as nodes** and the **streets connecting them as edges**. This visualization uses this concept to illustrate DFS. The **red and green lines** you see aren't just pretty colors; they represent the **"visited edges"** of the graph, showing the systematic exploration process of DFS. The algorithm attempts to go down a path, marking edges as it traverses. When it needs to backtrack, it might follow a previously explored edge in reverse, or simply pick a new unvisited path from a revisited node. The **vibrant yellow line** highlights the **computed path** – a potential route found by the algorithm, demonstrating the end result of its exploration. It's crucial to note the complexity differences here: while grid-based DFS is predictable, real-world road networks have varying street lengths, one-way systems, and traffic patterns. This can significantly impact the "speed" of DFS in practice, as edge traversal time (the time to "drive" a street) becomes a factor, a nuance often abstracted away in simpler graph problems.
